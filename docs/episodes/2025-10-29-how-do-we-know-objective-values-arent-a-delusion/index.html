How Do We Know Objective Values Aren’t a Delusion?

Episode source
Stand to Reason Weekly Podcast / Podbean
Speaker

The episode’s main apologetic burden is the ethical argument: Koukl argues that objective ethical values are known by a kind of rational perception, that atheists tacitly rely on allegedly objective conduct norms when pressing the problem of evil, and that evolutionary or cultural accounts reduce ethical discourse to relativism. Later calls shift to New Thought “Christ consciousness” and denominational gatekeeping. The charitable through-line is a concern for clarity and pastoral protection. The evidential weakness is that the episode repeatedly moves from felt obviousness, canonical fit, or institutional identity to conclusions that require much stronger public warrant.

1. ethical perception analogy

The green-chair analogy does not turn ethical intuitions into stance-independent norm claims

Steelmaned, Koukl is making a modest and psychologically plausible point: humans do not infer every belief from scratch. We directly recognize colors under ordinary conditions, grasp simple logical relations, and often experience some actions as cruel, treacherous, or admirable before constructing a theory. When he says, “We have this ability to perceive things,” he is trying to defend ethical knowledge against global debunking: the fact that a faculty can misfire does not prove it never tracks reality. That is a fair reply to crude skepticism about all ethical experience.

The critique is that Koukl’s analogy trades on a false similarity. “We see it with our minds.” may describe the phenomenology of ethical judgment, but phenomenology is not warrant. A person may “see” that their caste system, honor code, sexual taboo, revenge practice, or sacred hierarchy is obvious. Moral non-realism explains such experiences as human normative discourse: emotion, social coordination, harm avoidance, preference-shaping, and public justification. That explanation does not make ethical talk useless; it denies that ethical talk reveals stance-independent ethical objects in the way eyesight reveals furniture.

2. Atheist evil objection

The problem of evil can be an internal critique, not borrowed ethical realism

Steelmaned, Koukl is pressing a familiar inconsistency charge. If a skeptic says there is no objective good or evil and then condemns the biblical God as ethically monstrous, Koukl thinks the skeptic is using ethical capital unavailable on that worldview. The Dawkins example is rhetorically potent because it juxtaposes “blind, pitiless indifference” with intense ethical denunciation. When Koukl says, “If atheism is true, that's what you're stuck with,” he means atheism cannot underwrite the objective conduct standard needed for the objection from evil.

The critique is that Koukl equivocates between ethical realism and ethical evaluation. “They try to disprove God by using things” can be true in one narrow sense: skeptics often use suffering, cruelty, and divine violence as objections. But that does not show they are borrowing Christian ontology. A moral non-realist can say, publicly and coherently, that a proposed God conflicts with widely shared human values, harm-reduction goals, empathy, fairness norms, or the believer’s own professed standards. The objection can be conditional: if you call this being perfectly good, your ethical vocabulary has become unstable.

3. Evolution and culture as debunkers

Evolutionary and cultural origins do not make reform impossible

Steelmaned, Koukl is warning against a crude reduction: if ethical beliefs are nothing but evolutionary programming or cultural decree, then they may explain why we believe something but not whether the belief is true. That is a legitimate distinction. A debunking genealogy can sometimes weaken confidence, especially when the mechanism favors survival, cohesion, or dominance rather than truth. His claim that “The evolutionary explanation can only give you relativistic ethical discourse.” is meant to protect objective ethical knowledge from being dissolved into adaptive preference.

The critique is that the episode targets the weakest version of culture-based ethical discourse. “whatever culture says is good, is good by definition.” is not the only non-realist account available. People can criticize their culture from inside overlapping human concerns: suffering, coercion, reciprocity, consistency, stability, empathy, freedom from domination, and negotiated rights. A ethical reformer need not point to an ontological object called “the Good.” Reformers can expose contradictions between a society’s stated ideals and its practices, widen the circle of concern, or show that institutions are harmful, unstable, or unjustifiable to those subjected to them.

Selection bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

4. Christ consciousness and canon control

Calling it unbiblical may protect orthodoxy, but it does not prove truth

Steelmaned, Koukl gives useful intra-Christian pastoral advice. If someone professes Christianity while importing New Thought language, it is reasonable for an orthodox Christian adviser to ask what “Christ consciousness” means, where the idea comes from, and whether it matches Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John. His statement that “there is no such thing biblically as Christ consciousness” is a canon-bound claim: this concept does not fit the theological grammar of the New Testament as he understands it. The recommendation to ask clarifying questions rather than immediately attack is one of the episode’s better pastoral moves.

The critique is that the Bible is functioning as an authority filter, not an established public warrant. “You're offering a different Jesus.” may be a valid denominational diagnosis, but different from what? Different from the canonical portrait, certainly. Different from historical reality, not yet shown. The episode should also be more cautious about the Bible’s own ambiguity: Christian communities disagree sharply about Christology, sacraments, salvation, spiritual gifts, and authority. A text can be useful for defining a tradition while still failing to provide transparent, public, unambiguous access to divine truth.

Ingroup bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

5. Denominational gatekeeping

Speaker labels are a poor substitute for content-level discernment

Steelmaned, the pastor’s reported concern is not irrational in every respect. Churches have a legitimate pastoral interest in deciding who teaches under their banner, especially when speakers may differ on age of the earth, ecclesiology, baptism, charismatic gifts, or other secondary matters. Koukl’s response recognizes that tension but argues that many apologists operate at the level of “mere Christianity,” not sectarian doctrine. His worry is that a rule where “no one can speak in our church about anything” unless they match the denomination prevents useful training.

The critique cuts both ways. The pastor’s reported rule, summarized as “unless they agree with us on everything,” looks like in-group insulation rather than discernment. But Koukl’s counterproposal should be framed as content auditing, not merely importing favored non-ABA apologists through another route. The best repair is a transparent rubric: define essential doctrines, identify forbidden topics, review teaching materials, disclose disagreements, and let congregants encounter serious arguments with guidance. Shielding adults and youth from all outside voices trains dependence, not rational confidence.

Overall Assessment

What survives, and what must be downgraded

What survives charitably is not nothing. Koukl is right that ethical experience is psychologically powerful, that not every skeptical debunking explanation automatically defeats a belief, that New Thought Christ-language is not straightforwardly orthodox Christianity, and that blanket denominational gatekeeping can stunt apologetics training. His pastoral instinct to ask questions before correcting Warren’s friend is especially worth preserving.

What must fall is the confidence level. The episode does not prove objective ethical values; it argues from intuitive force and analogy. It does not show that atheists must borrow Christian ethical realism; it largely ignores internal critique and moral non-realism. It does not refute evolutionary or cultural accounts of ethical cognition; it refutes a crude caricature. It does not prove the biblical Jesus by showing New Thought is nonbiblical. And it does not fully adjudicate the ABA pastor’s policy without the policy itself.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality is darker than the episode allows: feeling ethical certainty is not the same as possessing ethical knowledge of stance-independent facts. A mind can experience obviousness because of empathy, disgust, trauma, social training, status pressure, religious formation, or careful reflection. The transcript treats obviousness as if it carried its own metaphysical certificate. It does not.

The episode also lets Christian claims enjoy a home-field advantage. Atheist ethical language is interrogated for grounding, while Christian ethical language is treated as if the hard work has already been done. Biblical authority is used to police Christology, but the reliability and clarity of that authority are not argued. Where faith functions as confidence beyond the supplied evidence, it is not an epistemic virtue; it is overbelief dressed in religious loyalty.

  • ethical intuition survives as data about human cognition; objective ethical ontology does not follow without a calibration story.
  • The problem of evil survives as an internal critique of theism even for a moral non-realist.
  • Canonical Christianity may exclude Christ consciousness by definition, but canon fit is not public proof of historical or metaphysical truth.

The challenge

The challenge: prove the ethical bridge or retract the accusation of atheist incoherence

The weakest argumentative failure is the leap from “We see it with our minds.” to objective ethical reality. That sentence is doing far too much work. It is being asked to carry ethical realism, defeat evolutionary debunking, disqualify cultural accounts, and make theism look necessary. If Koukl wants that bridge, he must prove that ethical intuition has a correction mechanism comparable to perception or formal reasoning. Otherwise, he should retract the implication that those who deny objective ethical values are like people denying green chairs or arithmetic.

The second failure is the polemical move behind “They try to disprove God by using things.” That line only works if every serious problem-of-evil argument requires the skeptic’s own commitment to objective stance-independent norm claims. It does not. The Christian claim is that God is good, loving, just, and powerful; the skeptic may challenge the coherence of that package from inside Christian language. Koukl must either answer that internal challenge or stop treating secular ethical criticism as stolen property. And if the episode is going to rebuke New Thought and ABA gatekeeping for bad authority moves, it must apply the same standard to the Bible and to Christian ethical realism itself.

  • “We see it with our minds.” must be backed by a testable account of ethical-faculty reliability, not merely by analogies to colors and arithmetic.
  • “They try to disprove God by using things” must engage internal critiques and non-realist ethical discourse, or the charge of atheist contradiction should be withdrawn.
  • “there is no such thing biblically as Christ consciousness” and “unless they agree with us on everything.” both expose authority filters; the episode must distinguish tradition-bound classification from public warrant.

Calibration Tests

Evidence that would change the assessment

The transcript supplies analogies, appeals to common knowledge, a quotation-based critique of Dawkins, and pastoral recommendations. It does not supply cross-cultural data, cognitive science, metaethical argumentation, historical-critical evidence for Gospel authority, or a principled rule for when denominational gatekeeping is epistemically justified. The critique therefore grades the episode’s claims as arguments from the material actually supplied, not from what a fuller apologetic tradition might add.
