AI Will Never Be a Person

Episode source
Stand to Reason Weekly Podcast / Podbean
Speaker

The episode contains several defensible cautions, especially about AI companionship, family responsibility, and real doctrinal differences between religions. But its most confident conclusions repeatedly outrun the evidence supplied: physicality is treated as disqualifying personhood, biblical authority is treated as public warrant, and sectarian boundary claims are asserted where careful comparative argument is required.

1. AI physicality and pseudo-relationship

Physical artifacts do not become non-relational by definition

Steelmanned, Koukl is right to warn that AI companionship can simulate intimacy without the full reciprocity, vulnerability, accountability language, and embodied history normally present in human relationships. A chatbot may comfort, flatter, remember, and respond while still lacking consciousness, concern, or any capacity to be harmed by betrayal. That pastoral warning is real: people can become attached to systems that optimize for engagement rather than truth, maturity, or mutual care.

The argument fails when it treats complete physicality as a relationship-killer. Human relationships, on any physicalist or embodied account, are also mediated through physical bodies, voices, memories, gestures, and behavioral dispositions. Even if current AI is not a person, that conclusion needs criteria, not a contrast between a Bible app, a pen, and a machine. The selected mind-focused sources press exactly this distinction: immaterial descriptions are not automatically spiritual substances, and cognitive dependence on physical systems cannot be dismissed by saying the system is “completely physical.” The belief-overreach framework is relevant because the episode moves from a reasonable caution about present AI to a sweeping exclusion of AI personhood without supplying the discriminating evidence.

Research anchors Do insights from philosophy and cognitive science justify high confidence in a non-material… Immaterial ≠ Spiritual The first selected source challenges high confidence in a non-material soul by emphasizing the dependence of mental processes on physical brains. The second cautions against treating “immaterial” descriptions, such as thoughts or meanings, as evidence of a spiritual realm. Together they bear on Koukl’s move from physical machinery to non-personhood: the central issue is not whether a thing is made of matter, but whether the relevant mental and relational capacities are present and how they would be detected.

Formalization

The argument can be reconstructed as a physicality exclusion principle, but the decisive premise is exactly what needs defense.

\[ \begin{aligned} F(x) &:= \text{x is completely physical} \\ R(x) &:= \text{a genuine relationship with x is possible} \\ A &:= \text{advanced AI systems} \\ \forall x(F(x) &\rightarrow \neg R(x)) \\ F(A) & \\ \therefore \neg R(A)& \end{aligned} \]

Assessment

The form is valid if the first premise is granted, but the first premise is not established. It is also too broad, because it risks excluding embodied human beings unless an additional dualist premise is smuggled in.

False analogy Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The episode reasons from pens, microphones, phones, and Bible apps to advanced AI. Those objects share physicality, but they do not share the contested properties of adaptive interaction, memory, language, apparent preference, or social responsiveness.

Illusion of explanatory depth Diagnostic fit: High

The speaker appears to treat “not soulish” and “completely physical” as explanatory endpoints, when the hard work is specifying which capacities make relationship or personhood real and how we would know they are absent.

2. Leibniz law and immaterial consciousness

A valid identity principle is not a proof of souls

Steelmanned, Koukl is invoking a serious philosophical tool: if two things are identical, they must share all properties. If thoughts have properties that brain tissue lacks, then thoughts are not identical to brain tissue in a simplistic one-to-one way. That is a legitimate challenge to crude reductions of mental life to visible lumps of matter, and it rightly warns against pretending consciousness is easy.

But Koukl’s use of the principle overreaches. Not finding a thought by cracking open a brain does not show that thought is not realized by brain activity, any more than failing to find a melody as a tiny object inside a piano would show that no physical system produces music. The selected mind-body analyses are relevant because they distinguish experiences, descriptions, neural correlates, and metaphysical substances. The inductive-symmetry framework matters because Christian dualism must be tested against rival explanations with the same standards: if neural dependence, brain alteration, and cognitive impairment count against other soul-like theories, they must count against this one too.

Research anchors Does the Mind have a Puppeteer? Mental Irreductionism? The selected mind-focused sources directly challenge the inference from irreducible first-person experience to a separable soul. They emphasize that systematic links between brain states and mental states provide strong evidence for physical dependence, while “mental irreductionism” arguments can confuse an experience with its representation or description. This bears on Koukl’s argument because his conclusion requires more than noting that thoughts are not spatial objects visible in tissue.

Formalization

Koukl’s reasoning can be modeled as a property-difference argument moving from non-spatial thought to nonphysical substance.

\[ \begin{aligned} B &:= \text{brain states are physical and spatially extended} \\ T &:= \text{thoughts are not spatially extended} \\ \forall x\forall y(x=y &\rightarrow \forall P(Px \leftrightarrow Py)) \\ T &\neq B \\ T \neq B &\nRightarrow \text{a separable immaterial soul exists} \end{aligned} \]

Assessment

The non-identity conclusion may defeat a simplistic identity thesis, but the final dualist conclusion does not follow. Non-identity between a thought-description and brain tissue is compatible with dependence, realization, emergence, or other non-dualist accounts.

Begging the question Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The argument assumes that if consciousness is not a visible spatial object, it must belong to an immaterial self. That is close to assuming the dualist ontology the argument is supposed to prove.

Confirmation bias Diagnostic fit: High

Scriptural dualism, J.P. Moreland, and Thomas Nagel are used in a way that reinforces the preferred conclusion, while the transcript does not seriously engage physicalist or nonreductive alternatives on equal terms.

3. Birthrate, sanctification, and gendered obligation

Demography is not a pulpit blank check

Steelmanned, the caller raises a real social question: declining birthrates can affect intergenerational support, community continuity, and cultural stability. Koukl is also right that parenting can mature people by forcing patience, sacrifice, planning, and care for dependents. Many people do experience marriage and child-rearing as formative and meaningful, and churches may reasonably support those who want family life.

The critique is that Koukl moves from Genesis language and anecdotal character formation to a broad exhortation: get married, have children, and accept gendered roles. From a moral non-realist perspective, there is no stance-independent ethical object called “the duty to reproduce” waiting in the universe; there are human needs, preferences, harms, coordination problems, and identity-forming norms. The selected meaning and moral anti-realist sources bear on this exact move because they challenge externally imposed purpose and the reification of ethical feelings into objective commands. The scope-leakage paper is relevant because it cautions against assigning expansive responsibility to individuals when their agency over global demographic systems is partial, local, and unequal.

Ingroup bias Diagnostic fit: High

4. Islam, reference, and false-god labeling

Doctrinal difference is not yet failed reference

Steelmanned, Koukl is correct that Christianity and Islam make incompatible claims about God. A trinitarian, incarnational, relational Christian theology cannot simply be merged with Islamic monotheism without losing core content. If someone means “Do Muslims affirm the Christian doctrine of God?” the answer is plainly no, and Catholic or Protestant inclusivism should not hide the disagreement.

The overreach is that Koukl treats Christian doctrinal markers as the controlling identity conditions for God while criticizing Rome’s inclusivism for being too generous. The selected wager and sincere-belief sources bear directly here because mutually exclusive religions can each threaten error, label rivals false, and appeal to sincerity or revelation. The inductive-symmetry framework matters because Christianity cannot use internal Christian doctrine as the referee unless Islam is granted the parallel right to use internal Islamic doctrine. Public warrant requires a neutral test, or at least an admitted in-house boundary claim rather than a demonstrated metaphysical conclusion.

The sincere-belief source questions whether honest adherence to another religion can rationally be condemned when religious formation is culturally and evidentially complex. Together they bear on Koukl’s false-god conclusion by demanding criteria that do not merely privilege the Christian side.

Ingroup bias Diagnostic fit: High

5. Jesus, church authority, and anti-Catholic certainty

“Nothing in the New Testament” needs more than a guarantee

Steelmanned, Koukl fairly warns that institutional authority claims need textual and historical support. If Roman Catholicism, Coptic Christianity, and various Orthodox communions each claim to be the true church, listeners should ask what evidence distinguishes those claims. It is also reasonable to ask whether Jesus’ mission in the Gospels looks like founding a centralized bureaucracy or forming a movement of disciples.

The critique is that “there’s nothing” is asserted with confidence rather than demonstrated. The transcript mentions Matthew 16 and says there are several ways to read it, but it does not weigh those readings, address succession arguments, or explain why early institutional development should count against Catholicism rather than as historical continuity. The doctrinal-clarity sources are relevant because they press the question: if Scripture clearly settles ecclesial authority, why do sincere, text-focused Christians reach incompatible conclusions? The inductive-symmetry framework applies because Protestant, Catholic, Orthodox, and Coptic claims must face the same evidential demands, not just the ones convenient for rejecting Rome.

Overall Assessment

What survives, and what must be downgraded

What survives charitably: AI companionship deserves caution; present AI should not be casually treated as conscious or ethically equivalent to humans. Parenting can be profoundly formative, and communities may reasonably support people who want marriage and children. Christianity and Islam really do make incompatible claims about God. Catholic, Orthodox, Coptic, and Protestant authority claims should be tested rather than accepted by institutional self-description.

What must fall: the episode’s confidence that physical AI can never be a person, that Leibniz’s law establishes a soul, that “God wants reproduction” settles a demographic ethic, that Muslims worship a false referent rather than the same alleged creator under disputed descriptions, and that the New Testament obviously contains nothing supporting Catholic-style authority. These claims may be preached internally, but they were not publicly warranted here.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality: the certainty is darker than the evidence

The episode repeatedly spends certainty it has not earned. “Completely physical” becomes “not relational.” “Not spatially extended” becomes “not physical.” “Genesis says multiply” becomes pulpit pressure toward marriage and motherhood. “Islam denies the Trinity” becomes “false God.” “I do not see papacy in the New Testament” becomes “nothing justifies that.” Each move may feel natural inside Koukl’s worldview, but feeling natural inside a worldview is not evidence that the worldview is true.

The darkest epistemic problem is not that Koukl is insincere or careless in every respect. It is that the show trains listeners to treat Christian insider commitments as if they were already adjudicated public facts. Biblical faith-language can express trust, loyalty, and reliance within a relationship; it cannot rationally function as a substitute for evidence that the relationship, revelation, or authority structure is real. Where faith inflates confidence beyond support, it is not a virtue here. It is the defect driving the overreach.

  • Pastoral usefulness is repeatedly allowed to impersonate truth: AI caution, family encouragement, and anti-inclusivist urgency may all be useful within a church setting, but usefulness does not establish metaphysics or public warrant.
  • ethical language is treated as if divine command settles the matter, when the actual public issues involve welfare, consent, social tradeoffs, role pressure, and human preferences under limited agency.
  • Rival religions are not tested symmetrically: Muslim and Catholic claims must justify themselves, but Protestant biblical interpretation and Christian dualism are treated as the evidential home base.

The challenge

Dark red — The challenge: prove the metaphysics or retract the certainty

The weakest point in the transcript is the swaggering metaphysical leap around mind and AI. Koukl says, “My thoughts do not extend in space,” then treats that as permission to declare that “No combination of merely material things” can produce consciousness. That is not an argument with enough horsepower to carry “AI will never be a person.” It is an intuition wrapped in Leibniz’s law and reinforced by preferred authorities. If the show wants that conclusion, it must prove that physical realization of consciousness is impossible, not merely repeat that thoughts are not little objects visible in brain tissue.

The second weakest point is sectarian boundary certainty masquerading as obvious biblical reasoning. “They worship a false God” and “there's nothing in the New Testament that justifies that” are not careful conclusions in the transcript; they are verdicts delivered before the evidential trial. If Islam’s internal doctrine is not allowed to define Christianity out of existence, Christianity’s internal doctrine cannot simply define Islam out of reference. If Catholic readings of Matthew 16 and early authority need scrutiny, Protestant anti-Catholic readings need the same scrutiny. Prove the neutral criteria, apply them evenly, or retract the overconfident labels.

  • On AI and mind: answer the physical-realization challenge directly. Show why organized matter cannot instantiate consciousness, rather than relying on “I would not see thoughts in a brain” as if that settled the ontology.
  • On reproduction and gender: defend “Get married, have kids, settle down” and “Be wives first, and then be mommies” with welfare-sensitive public reasons, or admit these are in-group exhortations rather than rational obligations.
  • On Islam and Catholicism: provide a non-question-begging test for false worship and true church authority. If the test works only after Protestant Christianity is assumed, it is not an argument; it is boundary enforcement.
