What Did Jesus Think About Homosexuality?

Episode source

Speaker

Greg Kokel argues that Jesus’ recorded silence on homosexuality is irrelevant, that Jesus’ Jewish and Genesis-based commitments clearly condemn same-sex relationships and modern gender claims, that Christianity uniquely supplies existential rescue, and that 1 Samuel does not teach ordinary Christians to learn an inner divine voice. The strongest material point is his narrow rebuttal that silence alone does not equal approval. The weakest move is the rapid expansion from selected biblical texts to sweeping public conclusions about sexuality, gender, meaning, and divine communication without supplying the evidential bridge those conclusions require.

1. Silence-to-approval rebuttal

Jesus’ silence does not settle Jesus’ approval

Charitably, Greg is right about the narrow logical point. If someone argues, “Jesus did not explicitly mention homosexuality in the surviving Gospel record; therefore Jesus approved of it,” that argument is weak. His analogies to other issues are meant to show that silence is not endorsement: a teacher can fail to address a subject in the preserved record without thereby approving it. As a rebuttal to a simplistic argument from silence, this survives.

The critique is that Greg overshoots. Jesus’ silence may not imply approval, but it can be relevant to how cautiously Christians should speak in Jesus’ name. The slavery analogy also cuts back against him: if the recorded Jesus tradition does not directly condemn slavery either, that is not a reason to rehabilitate slavery, but it does expose the danger of making “Jesus’ view” carry more modern emotional and normative force than the text can responsibly bear. From a moral non-realist perspective, the public question is not whether an ancient authority can be made to condemn a class of people’s relationships; it is whether a norm can be justified through human reasons such as harm, consent, coercion, flourishing, reciprocity, and social trust.

2. Torah-observant Jesus

Leviticus cannot be smuggled into Jesus without hermeneutical work

The strongest version of Greg’s claim is historically intelligible: Jesus was a first-century Jew who treated Israel’s Scriptures as authoritative, and Leviticus contains prohibitions of male same-sex intercourse. If the question is what background ethical world Jesus likely inhabited, Greg has a plausible starting point. A Torah-shaped Jesus probably did not approach sexual ethics as a modern liberal individualist.

But the inference is not as easy as Greg makes it sound. “Torah-observant Jew” does not automatically identify which parts of Torah Jesus would universalize, how he would rank them, how he would apply them outside Israel’s covenant setting, or how later Christians should translate them into public ethical claims. Greg also gestures toward “God wrote the Old Testament,” but that is an in-house theological premise, not public evidence. Even for Christians, the move from Leviticus to modern categories such as sexual orientation, consensual same-sex marriage, and gender identity requires more than pointing to a prohibition in a nearby ancient purity and holiness code.

Appeal to authority Diagnostic fit: Moderate

For believers who already accept Jesus and Torah as ethically authoritative, the appeal has internal force. As public argument, however, citing Jesus’ assumed endorsement of Leviticus substitutes authority for a shared account of why the condemned conduct is harmful, unjust, coercive, or socially destructive.

Authority bias Diagnostic fit: High

The argument leans heavily on the authority of Jesus, Torah, and divine authorship. That authority may guide insiders, but it can also suppress the need to justify the actual ethical classification in terms accessible to people who do not already grant the authority.

3. Matthew 19 maximalism

A divorce answer becomes a total sexual constitution

Greg’s strongest point is that Matthew 19 does portray Jesus appealing to Genesis when answering a question about divorce. In that passage, Jesus invokes male and female, a man leaving father and mother, union with his wife, one flesh, and permanence. For Christian sexual ethics, that is plainly relevant. It is reasonable for a Christian theologian to treat the passage as an important text about marriage, embodied sex, and covenantal permanence.

The overreach comes when Greg says this “covers all the bases” on homosexuality, same-sex marriage, transgender identity, gender dysphoria, and sex generally. A text can present a paradigmatic male-female marriage ideal without logically entailing a complete anthropology of gender identity or a public case against every consensual relationship outside that model. “Male and female” in a Genesis citation is not, by itself, an analysis of intersex conditions, trans experience, orientation, infertility, celibacy, adoption, or civil marriage. From a moral non-realist perspective, Greg may describe his community’s norm, but he has not shown why outsiders should treat that norm as a publicly justified constraint on other people’s lives.

4. Life-vest meaning apologetic

The parable turns rival lives into drowning caricatures

The parable Greg reads is pastorally understandable as Christian testimony. It presents Christianity as rest, grace, and rescue rather than anxious self-salvation. In that limited sense, it conveys how many Christians experience their faith: not as a private achievement, but as dependence on something they believe is outside themselves. As devotional rhetoric, the image of a life preserver captures a genuine feeling of security.

As an argument, however, the parable simply assigns the Christian the rescue device and assigns the atheist and spiritualist eventual drowning. That is not a demonstration; it is narrative rigging. The atheist is made aimless, the spiritualist exhausted, and the Christian peacefully secure, but those characterizations are not established by the transcript. Non-theists can have durable meaning, communal obligations, love, projects, beauty, and courage without positing a cosmic rescuer. From a moral non-realist and naturalistic perspective, meaning is not discovered as an ontological object floating in the sea; it is made, negotiated, embodied, revised, and shared by human agents.

Halo effect Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The emotional attractiveness of Christian peace casts a halo over the truth claim. Because the Christian is portrayed as calm and compassionate, the worldview is made to appear epistemically superior without separate evidential support.

5. Samuel and divine voice

Samuel is a better anti-proof-text than proof-text

This is one of Greg’s stronger exegetical moments. He argues that 1 Samuel is not a manual for ordinary believers learning to recognize an inner divine voice. The text concerns a rare revelatory setting, a child who did not yet know the Lord, an ungodly Eli, and the commissioning of Samuel as a prophet. That reading is far more context-sensitive than the popular devotional use he criticizes.

Still, Greg’s counterargument has its own overconfidence. His statement that “God did not fail” is driven partly by prior theology about what God can or cannot do, not only by the narrative. He is right that private experiences cannot become standards for Christian practice merely because someone reports them. But if he allows that “God could do whatever he wants,” then he owes a consistent evidential test for alleged divine communication: what would distinguish God, self-suggestion, social expectation, memory editing, coincidence, or pastoral pressure? Biblical trust-language can express relational reliance, but when faith becomes confidence that God has spoken without testable warrant, it is an epistemic defect, not a virtue.

Overall Assessment

What survives and what must be downgraded

What survives charitably: Greg’s rebuttal to the crude silence-to-approval argument is basically right, and his correction of the Samuel proof-text is largely responsible. Silence alone does not prove Jesus approved of a practice, and 1 Samuel is much more naturally read as prophetic commissioning than as a tutorial in private voice recognition.

What must be downgraded: the confidence that Matthew 19 “covers all the bases” on homosexuality, same-sex marriage, transgender identity, and gender dysphoria is far too high for the evidence presented. The Leviticus-to-Jesus argument is plausible as a historical probability about Jesus’ Jewish background, but it is not a complete public ethical argument. The life-vest parable is testimony and rhetoric, not evidence that Christianity is true or that atheists and spiritualists are existentially doomed. Greg’s confidence should be high only for the narrow silence rebuttal and the anti-proof-text reading of Samuel, moderate for Jesus’ likely inherited Jewish sexual assumptions, and low for the sweeping modern applications and worldview caricatures.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality is that this episode repeatedly turns internal Christian authority into more certainty than the evidence can bear. A search query is not motive evidence. A Levitical citation is not a full hermeneutic. A Genesis marriage text is not a comprehensive gender anthropology. A moving life-vest image is not a flotation test. And a doctrinal conviction that God cannot fail is not the same thing as an argument extracted from the Samuel narrative itself.

Faith may function relationally as trust, loyalty, and reliance within Christian practice. But where faith becomes a basis for confidence that outruns evidence, it is not an epistemic virtue. It is overbelief. The episode is at its best when it asks people to read the text carefully. It is at its worst when it uses the text as a shortcut to settle complex ethical and metaphysical claims without public warrant.

  • The narrow logical claim “silence does not imply approval” survives; the broader claim “silence is irrelevant” should be reduced.
  • The move from “he made them male and female” to “all modern gender and sexuality questions are unmistakably settled” is not demonstrated.
  • The parable about drowning rivals may comfort insiders, but it supplies no independent evidence that the Christian “life vest” exists or that non-Christians lack meaning.

The challenge

The challenge: textual control is not proof

Dark red warning: the weakest argumentative failure in this episode is the attempt to make Jesus do more work than the transcript’s evidence permits. If Greg wants to say, “Jesus never said anything about slavery” blocks a bad argument from silence, fine. But he does not get to pivot from that modest point to “One man with one woman becoming one flesh for one lifetime” and announce that homosexuality, same-sex marriage, transgender identity, and gender dysphoria have all been unmistakably handled. That is not exegesis finishing the job; that is a slogan being treated as a verdict.

The episode must prove or retract its strongest condemnatory confidence. It must prove that Leviticus transfers through Jesus into a timeless public conduct rule with a clear scope. It must prove that “he made them male and female” excludes the actual contemporary cases Greg names rather than merely stating a Genesis marriage paradigm. It must prove that the atheist who “will just float until he can’t float anymore” is not a caricature. If it cannot do those things, then it should retract the posture of certainty and admit that it has offered internal Christian boundary-marking, not a publicly warranted argument against other people’s relationships, identities, or sources of meaning.

  • The motive claim about critics being self-serving must be evidenced or dropped; analytics show interest in Jesus and homosexuality, not the inner psychology of searchers.
  • The Matthew 19 expansion must answer exactly how a divorce teaching becomes a complete sexual and gender taxonomy; “covers all the bases” is the claim, not the proof.
  • The life-vest parable must stop pretending that Christian comfort establishes Christian truth; it must either supply independent evidence for the rescuer or admit it is only testimony.
