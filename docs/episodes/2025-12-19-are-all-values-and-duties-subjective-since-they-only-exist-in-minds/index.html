Are All Values and Duties Subjective Since They Only Exist in Minds?

This episode’s strongest moments are modest ones: Koukl correctly resists a crude inference from “nonphysical” to “not objective,” reads Ephesians and Romans as connected Pauline themes rather than isolated slogans, and warns that support for Israel need not mean approving every Israeli action. The trouble is that he repeatedly turns those limited points into far heavier conclusions: divine ethical realism, God as the only possible explanation of evil, eternal destruction as a contrastive display of mercy, ancient covenant as modern political warrant, and biblical text as the controlling answer to whether God is good.

1. Mind-dependence and ethical objectivity

Nonphysical concepts do not rescue divine ethical realism

Steelmanned, Koukl’s reply to the caller has a legitimate target. The caller’s argument appears to move too quickly from “ethical concepts are not physical things” to “therefore they cannot be objective.” Koukl is right that physicality and objectivity are not identical categories. Mathematical truths, logical relations, fictional propositions, institutional facts, and conceptual structures can be discussed without being little material objects sitting in space. So his correction of a crude physicalist premise is a real point: if the challenger meant that only physical objects can be objectively discussed, the challenge is underargued.

But Koukl’s stronger conclusion does not follow from that correction. Showing that nonphysical claims can be objective does not show that ethical claims are objective, that ethical wrongness is a property in the world, or that the property is grounded in God’s character. From a moral non-realist perspective, ethical language is better understood as human normative discourse: harm aversion, social coordination, emotional pressure, preference negotiation, and public justification. Koukl’s move from “not necessarily physical” to “grounded in God’s unchanging character” supplies a metaphysical object called goodness where the episode has not provided an epistemology, a mechanism of access, or a way to adjudicate sincere ethical disagreement.

The first premise is plausible, but the conclusion overreaches. At most, the argument shows that nonphysicality does not automatically entail subjectivity. It does not show that ethical properties exist, that God exists, or that God’s character is the successful ground of obligation.

Equivocation Diagnostic fit: High

The argument shifts between several senses of objectivity: not physical, not merely individual preference, stable in God, and ethically binding. Those are not equivalent. Treating them as if they transfer automatically lets the conclusion appear stronger than the premises warrant.

Illusion of explanatory depth Diagnostic fit: High

The language of grounding in God’s character sounds architecturally complete, but the episode does not explain the key machinery: how character becomes obligation, how humans reliably access it, or how conflicting Christian ethical judgments are corrected.

2. Evil as proof of a divine conduct standard

The problem of evil does not smuggle God into the premises

Steelmanned, Koukl is pressing a familiar challenge: when people protest evil, they often speak as though suffering, cruelty, and injustice are not merely disliked but genuinely wrong. The theist can fairly ask what kind of worldview makes sense of that robust ethical language. If someone says rape, torture, or genocide are not just unpleasant but evil, Koukl wants to know what grounds the standard by which that judgment is made. That is a serious question for any view that talks as though ethical condemnation has more force than personal taste.

The critique is that Koukl turns that fair question into a false dilemma. The problem of evil can be framed internally: if Christians claim God is all-good, all-knowing, and all-powerful, then observed suffering pressures that claim using Christianity’s own ethical and theological commitments. A moral non-realist can also condemn rape through harm, coercion, social trust, empathy, trauma, and public norms without positing an objective ethical substance. Koukl’s claim that only a divine foundation can make sense of evil confuses ethical seriousness with ethical realism and then ethical realism with Christian theism.

3. Wrath as a backdrop for mercy

Judgment-by-contrast explains Paul better than it vindicates God

Steelmanned, Koukl’s textual answer is plausible as an interpretation of the two Pauline passages. Ephesians emphasizes grace and kindness toward the saved; Romans 9 adds the darker contrast of wrath, patience, destruction, and mercy. Koukl’s point is that Paul is not contradicting himself but describing the same divine glory from different angles. One passage isolates rescue; the other says judgment makes mercy more visible by contrast. As a piece of intra-Pauline exegesis, that is at least coherent.

But the explanation does not become a ethical vindication claim just because it is textually tidy. Saying that punishment displays mercy by contrast risks turning the damned into background scenery for the blessed. If a ruler harmed some subjects so favored subjects could better appreciate pardon, we would not call that obviously good; we would ask whether the harmed persons were being instrumentalized. The episode separates textual assertion from public warrant too weakly. Even if Paul teaches this, the claim that eternal destruction magnifies grace remains ethically and rationally burdened.

Appeal to authority Diagnostic fit: Moderate

Authority bias Diagnostic fit: High

Because Romans 9 is treated as authoritative, the disturbing ethical implications are softened into a textual-management problem. The authority of the passage reduces pressure to ask whether the described arrangement is defensible.

4. Abrahamic covenant and modern Israel

Ancient land promise is not yet public political warrant

Steelmanned, Koukl does not offer the crudest version of Christian Zionism. He explicitly distinguishes affirming Israel’s role from approving every action of the Israeli government, and he appeals to the biblical prophets as evidence that one can be pro-Israel while criticizing Israel’s conduct. His biblical chain is also intelligible: Genesis promises Abraham land and descendants; the promise is narrowed through Isaac and Jacob; the Abrahamic covenant is not, in his view, abrogated by the new covenant; therefore national Israel retains theological significance.

The critique is that the argument moves from an ancient covenantal text to modern political conclusions without enough intervening work. Even if Genesis records a land promise, it does not automatically settle modern borders, state legitimacy, military policy, Palestinian claims, diaspora identity, or Christian civic obligation. Koukl’s summary sometimes treats the biblical frame as if it can bypass ordinary historical and ethical scrutiny. From a public-reason perspective, a contested theological interpretation cannot by itself establish rightful land claims for everyone affected by the policy.

5. God not saving everyone

Text-first theology does not answer the ethical challenge

Steelmanned, Koukl rightly notes that the Arminian-Calvinist divide does not eliminate every version of the problem. Even if God merely foreknows free rejection rather than decreeing reprobation, one can still ask why God creates people he knows will end in destruction. Koukl also has a coherent insider method: if someone accepts Scripture as authoritative, then one should first ask what the text teaches and then wrestle with emotional and philosophical difficulties in light of that teaching.

But as an apologetic response to doubt, the move is circular and pastorally risky. If the issue is whether the God of the Bible is good, then “the text says so” cannot be the final court of appeal unless the text’s normative authority is already granted. Koukl’s answer also treats trust in an undisclosed divine reason as reasonable because the system says God is good. That is epistemic faith functioning as confidence beyond supplied evidence, and in rational terms it is a defect, not a virtue. Biblical faith-language may express trust, reliance, and loyalty; it does not provide independent evidence that eternal destruction is good.

Overall Assessment

What survives, and what confidence must fall

What survives charitably is limited but real. Koukl is right that nonphysicality is not the same as subjectivity. He gives a plausible intra-Pauline reading of Ephesians and Romans. He avoids the worst form of political-theological absolutism by allowing criticism of Israel. He also correctly notes that Arminianism does not erase every version of the question of why God creates those who will be lost.

What does not survive is the episode’s confidence inflation. The ethical argument does not establish God merely because people condemn evil. Divine character does not become allegedly objective conduct norms simply by being fixed. Romans 9 may explain Paul’s contrast, but it does not vindicate eternal destruction. Genesis may matter inside a theological system, but it does not by itself settle modern land politics. A text-first method may guide insiders, but it does not answer a doubter asking whether the text’s God is good in the first place. Those confidence levels must fall sharply.

The epistemic reality

Dark: The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality is that this episode repeatedly converts internal Christian grammar into public warrant without paying the bridge toll. “God’s character,” “the Abrahamic covenant,” “vessels of wrath,” and “what the text teaches” all function as if naming the doctrine were enough to justify the conclusion. It is not. A doctrine can be sincere, traditional, pastorally useful, and textually motivated while still lacking rational warrant for outsiders or doubters.

The darkest failure is the treatment of faith-shaped trust as though it solves evidential pressure. Trust, reliance, and loyalty can be meaningful relational attitudes after a person has adequate reason to trust. But when confidence in hidden divine reasons is offered where evidence is missing, faith is not an epistemic virtue. It is overbelief wearing devotional clothing.

  • The ethical sections defeat crude subjectivism but do not establish divine ethical realism.
  • The Romans 9 sections achieve textual management, not ethical vindication claim.
  • The Zionism discussion needs historical, legal, and ethical bridges before ancient covenant becomes modern obligation.

The challenge

Dark red: The challenge — prove the bridges or retract the confidence

The weakest move in the transcript is the repeated slide from assertion to entitlement. “The only way to make sense of it,” “the punishment shows the greatness of the mercy by contrast,” and “we have an obligation in light of the Abrahamic covenant” are not conclusions earned in the episode; they are conclusions imported from the system. Koukl must prove that ethical outrage requires divine ontology, that destruction can righteously function as contrast material for mercy, and that Genesis can bear the weight of modern political obligation. If he cannot, those claims should be retracted or radically downgraded.

The second weakest move is telling the doubter that “The right question is, what does the text teach” when the doubter is asking whether the God depicted by the text is good. That is not pastoral courage; it is an epistemic circle. The episode must either provide independent reasons to trust the text’s portrayal of divine goodness or admit that its answer works only for those already inside the authority structure. If the case depends on faith in hidden reasons, say so plainly, and stop presenting that faith as rational resolution.

  • On ethical discourse: prove that objective wrongness exists and that God’s character uniquely grounds it, or stop treating the caller’s flawed physicalism as a victory for Christian ethical realism.
  • On judgment: prove that “vessels of wrath prepared for destruction” can be reconciled with proportional justice and non-instrumentalization, or stop calling destruction a display of mercy as if the phrase solves the horror.
  • On Israel: define exactly what Christian obligation requires today, what evidence connects ancient covenant to modern state policy, and what would count as Israel acting wrongly enough to forfeit support in a particular case.
