Should Praying for Miracles Be an Integral Part of Evangelism?

Episode source
Stand to Reason Weekly Podcast / Podbean

This episode is at its best when Koukl refuses a simplistic miracle-on-demand theology and admits that some biblical passages around prayer and greater works remain hard to interpret. It is weakest when it lets anecdotal miracle reports, broad claims about Christian cultural achievement, inherited sin anthropology, supernatural story-explanation, and atonement bookkeeping do more epistemic work than the supplied evidence can bear.

Critique Framework

How this page evaluates the episode

Calibration

Belief should track evidential support rather than identity, comfort, group pressure, or epistemic faith; trust or loyalty is not evidence for truth.

Symmetry

Inductive permission granted to Christianity must be granted to parallel claims unless a real differentiator is supplied.

Architecture

A source of conduct rules is not yet a public normative framework with access, binding force, scope, and repair.

Alternatives

Christian explanations must compete with secular, pluralist, psychological, and social explanations.

Bounded Agency

Concern should become proportionate action where agency exists, not inflated cosmic responsibility.

1. Miracle prayer as evangelistic confirmation

Anecdotal signs cannot carry public evidential weight

The strongest version of Koukl’s miracle-prayer claim is not a crude health-and-wealth promise. He distinguishes descriptive biblical episodes from universal prescription, admits uncertainty about some passages, and lands pastorally: pray for the sick, preach the gospel, and do not make God a vending machine. He also reports a pattern among Muslim converts, where supernatural experiences seem to accompany conversion more often than in other groups. Charitably, that is an attempt to honor testimony without turning every evangelistic encounter into a miracle performance.

The critique is not that prayer is emotionally useless or that no reported healing can ever be investigated. It is that the episode does not give listeners a way to tell the difference between God selectively answering prayer and ordinary variance plus selective memory. Relational trust-language, such as continuing to trust God amid suffering, may function as loyalty or coping commitment within a community. But if that trust becomes a basis for believing that God is actually intervening, it is epistemically defective unless the outcomes differ from what would be expected without a prayer-answering agent.

2. John 14 and corporate greater works

A failed literal reading is rescued by an unsupported cultural expansion

Koukl notices a real problem in the text: if John 14 means each believer will individually perform miracles greater than Jesus, the claim appears false. His charitable repair is to read the promise corporately, as referring to the expanded historical impact of the church after Jesus’ departure. That reading is at least more plausible than pretending every Christian outdoes Jesus’ miracles, and Koukl is right to resist a facile proof-texting of miracle ministry from the passage.

The larger problem is the historical overreach attached to the interpretation. Christian institutions may have contributed to education, medicine, literacy, and reform movements; those contributions can be acknowledged without converting them into proof that John 14 was fulfilled or that Christianity is true. Social benefit is not the same as metaphysical validation. A non-Christian movement could also produce schools, hospitals, or liberation campaigns without thereby proving its supernatural claims. The episode needs comparative historical evidence and textual argument, not merely a sweeping success narrative.

3. Human nature, guilt, and ethical brokenness

Guilt is not evidence of a cosmic fall

Koukl’s anthropology is more nuanced than a simple denial that humans can do good. He distinguishes ontological value, especially being made in God’s image, from behavioral corruption, and he points to obvious human cruelty as evidence that something is deeply wrong with us. Charitably, this captures a familiar human experience: people are capable of cooperation, care, and beauty, but also deception, violence, selfishness, and cruelty. Any serious anthropology must account for both sides.

From a moral non-realist perspective, the guilt argument is especially weak. Guilt can be understood as a socially shaped emotional response involving harm, norm violation, empathy, reputation, fear, and internalized expectations. It is often useful for repair and coordination, but it is not a detector of stance-independent stance-independent norm claims. Koukl’s appeal to allegedly objective conduct norms and guilt may work rhetorically for hearers already inside a Christian ethical frame, but public justification requires more than the fact that people feel bad after doing things their community condemns or their empathy recognizes as harmful.

4. Talking snake and supernatural narration

A supernatural add-on explains the story only after the story is trusted

Koukl handles the caller’s question with some intellectual modesty. He admits that it is odd that Eve is not portrayed as surprised by a talking snake, and he does not pretend to know Eve’s psychology. His face-value reading is internally simple: snakes do not normally talk, so if the story is historical, a supernatural agent must have enabled it. Within a Bible-accepting framework, that answer is coherent enough as an in-house harmonization.

The episode therefore distinguishes poorly between narrative explanation and historical warrant. A story can have an internally available explanation without becoming publicly credible. Mythic, symbolic, etiological, or theological narrative functions are live alternatives whenever a text contains impossible animal speech and minimal independent corroboration. Koukl’s answer may satisfy a believer who asks how the story works if Genesis is true. It does not give a non-insider reason to think a serpent actually spoke or that Satan actually acted through it.

5. Limited atonement and payment logic

The bookkeeping is tidy only after the ethical machinery is assumed

Koukl’s atonement answer is clearer than many popular treatments because he distinguishes availability from application. His strongest point is logical: if Christ’s death actually pays the sins of every person in the same applied sense, universalism seems to follow; if not everyone is saved, then the atonement’s saving application is limited to those who meet the stated condition. That is a coherent internal response to a common objection, and it avoids some confusion around the word for.

The faith issue also needs cleaning up. If faith means trust, reliance, or loyalty toward Jesus after one already has adequate warrant, it may describe a relational commitment rather than evidence. But if faith functions as the basis for believing the atonement story or for maintaining confidence beyond the evidence, it is a rational defect. From a moral non-realist angle, talk of debt, guilt, payment, and deserved punishment is human normative and legal metaphor, not an discovered object called justice. The episode must justify why this metaphor should govern reality rather than merely organize Christian soteriology.

Formalization

Koukl’s internal logic can be represented cleanly, but the premises need defense.

\[ (P(x) \to F(x)) \land \neg F(n) \Rightarrow \neg P(n);\quad P(x) \text{ is applied payment, not mere provision} \]

Assessment

If applied payment P entails forgiveness F, and a nonbeliever n is not forgiven, then applied payment was not made for n. The form is valid, but the theological meaning of payment, forgiveness, and faith remains under-argued.

False analogy Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The debt analogy makes atonement feel legally obvious, but financial payment and ethical culpability are not obviously equivalent, especially when punishment is transferred to an innocent substitute.

Motivated reasoning Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The reasoning is shaped to avoid universalism while preserving inherited atonement commitments. That may be internally consistent, but the episode does not seriously test whether the underlying model should be rejected.

Overall Assessment

Overall assessment: caution survives, confidence must fall

Several parts of the episode survive charitably. Koukl is right to resist simplistic miracle guarantees, right to notice that John 14 is difficult, right that human beings are ethically and psychologically mixed, right that the talking-snake question is fair, and right that atonement debates often turn on ambiguous uses of for. Those are real strengths.

But the confidence levels must fall wherever the episode moves from internal Christian interpretation to public warrant. Contemporary miracle reports need documentation and comparison. Corporate greater-works claims need textual and historical support. Guilt does not prove allegedly objective conduct norms or the fall. A supernatural explanation of a talking snake does not establish the event. Limited atonement’s internal logic does not vindicate the ethical machinery of penal substitution.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality: comforting explanations are not evidence

The dark epistemic reality is that the episode repeatedly offers believers a way to keep confidence without paying the evidential cost of that confidence. If prayer works, God answered. If prayer fails, God has a different purpose. If a miracle text looks false literally, it becomes corporate cultural achievement. If guilt is felt, it becomes proof of real guilt. If a snake talks, invisible agency is invoked. If atonement sounds universal, for is narrowed until the system survives.

That is not careful public reasoning; it is theological insulation unless each move is exposed to rival explanations and possible defeat. Faith as trust may be a relational stance, but faith as belief-maintenance beyond evidence is irrational. Pastoral usefulness can be humane, but it cannot be smuggled in as historical, empirical, ethical, or metaphysical warrant.

  • Separate testimony from documentation: a moving conversion or healing story is not yet evidence that a supernatural agent acted.
  • Separate ethical emotion from ethical ontology: guilt, outrage, and compassion are human experiences, not automatic proof of objective stance-independent norm claims.
  • Separate internal coherence from truth: a doctrine can be made consistent by definitions while still lacking public justification.

The challenge

The challenge: prove the miracle machinery or retract the overreach

The weakest move in the episode is the repeated escape into protected explanations. The claim that supernatural factors occur in Muslim conversions because most answers are yes must either be documented with failures included or lowered to anecdote. The line if he answers, fine, if he doesn't, trust God may be pastorally serviceable, but as an evidential policy it is a blank check. A claim that wins on healing and wins on non-healing has not earned the right to call itself evidence.

The other weak point is the inflation of Christian insider claims into public conclusions. That's how the world got literate is not a careful historical argument; it is apologetic overreach unless backed by comparative data. Maybe we feel guilty because we are guilty is not ethical philosophy; it is a leap from emotion to ontology. If you pay the debt, it's paid is not a theory of justice; it is a metaphor being treated like machinery. The episode must prove these moves with public standards or retract them to in-house theology.

  • Miracle evangelism: document the alleged pattern behind most of the time the answer is yes with denominators, failures, medical records, and rival-religion comparisons, or stop treating it as confirmatory.
  • Greater works and literacy: prove the corporate John 14 reading and the historical claim behind That's how the world got literate, or reduce it to an appreciative but non-evidential Christian narrative.
  • Guilt and atonement: show why guilt detects objective divine culpability and why innocent-substitute debt payment is ethically coherent, or admit that these are theological metaphors without public warrant.

Calibration Tests

Evidence that would change the assessment

The episode supplies biblical references, personal impressions, ministry anecdotes, and theological reasoning. It does not supply controlled miracle documentation, historical data for civilizational claims, a public ethical ontology, independent evidence for Genesis supernaturalism, or a fully defended account of penal substitution. Those gaps determine the confidence limits below.

◉ What evidence is actually supplied in the steelmanned condensed argument.
◉ What evidence is asserted but not presented.
◉ What rival explanations or rival worldviews must be compared.
◉ Whether the confidence expressed exceeds the evidence supplied, especially where faith is invoked as a substitute for evidence.
◉ Which assumptions are doing hidden work.
◉ Whether the claim is primarily pastoral, psychological, ethical/normative, historical, metaphysical, or evidential.

Ensure your analysis exhaustively addresses the following vulnerabilities in the original claims:
◉ Worldview Totalization: Examine whether Christianity is asserted as a map of all reality rather than argued to be the uniquely accurate map of all reality.
◉ Faith and Evidence Categories: Distinguish relational trust, reliance, or loyalty from epistemic faith; treat epistemic faith as intrinsically irrational wherever it functions as a basis for belief rather than evidence-proportionate confidence.
◉ Private-to-Public Shift: Test whether moving from personal faith to public worldview smuggles in authority claims that still require public justification.
◉ Evidence-Proportionate Belief: Assess whether claims about resurrection, lordship, creation, Fall, redemption, and final restoration receive enough evidence to justify the confidence placed in them.
◉ Pastoral Usefulness Versus Truth: Evaluate whether the existential usefulness of hope, identity, and calling is being treated as evidence that the worldview is true.
◉ Narrative Closure: Analyze whether a promised ending functions as a circular story-ending premise that predetermines the interpretation of present events.
◉ Insider Authority: Examine whether appeals to Scripture and Christian tradition establish the claims only for insiders who already grant those sources authority.
◉ Resurrection Evidence Gap: Evaluate moves from mentioning evidence to treating resurrection as a central reality when the actual evidence is not presented.
◉ Lordship Claim Expansion: Assess whether lordship claims are argued as public facts or merely proclaimed as theological commitments.
◉ Equivocation Risk: Check for shifts in the meanings of worldview, hope, truth, Lord, good, fallen, redeemed, identity, and calling.
◉ Cultural-Moment Framing: Examine whether culture-war examples create a false dilemma, strawman, or asymmetric framing.
◉ Analogy Limits: Test whether literary or pastoral analogies legitimately support public conclusions.
◉ Historical Selectivity: Evaluate uses of church-history examples for cherry-picking, survivorship bias, halo effect, or hasty generalization.
◉ Normative-Claim Threshold: Ask whether theological categories generate determinate conduct guidance without additional contested premises.
◉ Inductive Symmetry: Compare the standards used to accept Christian explanatory claims with the standards required for rival worldviews.
◉ Scope Leakage: Identify moves from this helps Christian students live with hope and purpose to therefore Christianity is true or uniquely adequate.
◉ Burden of Proof and Special Pleading: Determine whether rival views are asked to justify themselves while Christian claims are exempted from comparable scrutiny.
◉ Non Sequitur Risk: Identify conclusions that do not follow from premises, especially from prosocial motivation to metaphysical truth or from scriptural narrative to public epistemic warrant.

Conclude with:
◉ The strongest charitable version of the argument after repair.
◉ The minimum evidence and reasoning required for the repaired version to justify stronger confidence.
◉ A short list of claims that should be downgraded in confidence if rational belief must map to the degree of relevant evidence.
