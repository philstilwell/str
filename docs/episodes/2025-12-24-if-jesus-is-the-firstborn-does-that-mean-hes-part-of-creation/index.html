If Jesus Is the Firstborn, Does That Mean He’s Part of Creation?

This episode is strongest when it slows down a caller’s anti-Trinitarian reading of Colossians 1:15 and asks what “firstborn” means in context. Its weaknesses appear when internal textual coherence is treated as metaphysical proof, when devotional usefulness is allowed to outrun historical warrant, and when a narrative detail about Elizabeth is inflated into public confirmation of a virgin conception.

1. Prototokos as status, not creaturehood

“Firstborn” can plausibly mean preeminence, but the episode overstates the lexical repair

Koukl’s strongest point is that the caller’s opponent is too quick to read the English phrase “firstborn of creation” as if “first” simply modifies “born” in a biological or created-order sense. He argues that the Greek term functions as a single expression and that biblical usage can mark rank, inheritance, or preeminence rather than literal birth order. That is a serious exegetical move, and it charitably answers the caller’s concern: Colossians 1:15 need not mean Jesus is the first created being if the term is status language.

The weakness is that the episode treats the favored reading as nearly forced while offering only improvised cross-references and a harmonizing pressure from nearby verses. If the text is divinely important, the ambiguity itself matters: a phrase that repeatedly misleads ordinary readers into a major Christological dispute is not an obvious mark of doctrinal clarity. The anti-Trinitarian inference may be weaker than the caller feared, but Koukl has not shown that his own inference deserves the certainty he implies.

The inference is reasonable as a rebuttal to the simplistic “firstborn means first created” objection. It becomes overreach when semantic plausibility is treated as decisive proof without a broader lexical survey and without addressing why a divinely clear text would permit such durable confusion.

Cherry picking Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The episode selects biblical examples that support the preeminence sense, but it does not survey the full usage range or show that disconfirming cases are absent or irrelevant.

Confirmation bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

Koukl’s Trinitarian commitment makes the status reading attractive, and the discussion moves quickly from compatible examples to a settled conclusion without testing the strongest rival construal.

2. Creator-through-whom-everything-exists

The uncreated-creator inference is internally valid only if the textual premises are already granted

Koukl’s second major move is logically clean within the Christian canon: if every created thing came into being through Christ, then Christ cannot himself be one of the created things. He also appeals to John 1:3 as a parallel that excludes any created entity from existing apart from the Word. Charitably, this is the most powerful part of his answer to the caller. If those texts are authoritative, harmonized, and universal in scope, the anti-Trinitarian reading faces a real internal problem.

But the episode then jumps from an internal scriptural inference to ontological confidence: Jesus is “the uncreated creator,” therefore divine. That conclusion is not publicly warranted merely by citing Colossians and John. It requires independent reasons to trust these documents as reliable access to divine metaphysics, and it requires addressing the coherence costs of the broader incarnation doctrine. A non-Christian listener can grant the internal conditional while withholding belief in the external reality.

Assessment

The validity is not the problem; the warrant for the premises is. The episode provides an internal doctrinal argument, not a publicly sufficient demonstration that the metaphysical conclusion is true.

Begging the question Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The conclusion is secured partly by assuming that the relevant Christian texts are mutually authoritative and should be harmonized toward Trinitarian doctrine, which is precisely what a non-Trinitarian or non-Christian interlocutor may dispute.

Authority bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The reasoning leans heavily on canonical authority. For listeners already committed to the New Testament, that authority carries weight; for outsiders, it cannot substitute for independent warrant.

3. Devotional films and the “do no harm” filter

Edification is not the same thing as historical warrant

Koukl’s rule for Bible films is pastoral and intelligible: a film need not reproduce every detail perfectly to be spiritually or emotionally useful, but it should not distort Jesus or the theological center of the story. That is a fair distinction between artistic dramatization and damaging misrepresentation. He is also candid that beloved portrayals, including Christmas films, contain inaccuracies. The charitable reading is that he wants viewers to benefit devotionally without becoming pedants about every set-design choice.

Still, “no harm” is too vague to carry the epistemic load Koukl gives it. From a moral non-realist perspective, harm here is not an objective ethical property floating in the universe; it is a human assessment about pedagogy, truthfulness, trust, and downstream belief formation. Emotional power can be useful, but it can also launder weak historical claims into confidence. A story that feels reverent has not thereby become reliable.

4. The Bethlehem lodging reconstruction

The kataluma correction is plausible, but the transcript gives assertions where evidence is needed

Koukl offers a concrete correction to the familiar Christmas-card scene: Bethlehem probably did not have commercial inns, Joseph and Mary likely stayed with relatives, the “inn” was better understood as a guest room, and the animals occupied the lower part of the house. This is a useful reminder that traditional imagery can drift far from historical reconstruction. He also makes a commendable methodological point by recommending an LDS-produced film because he thinks its depiction is accurate; source identity alone should not decide truth.

The problem is the level of certainty. “There were no inns in Bethlehem” and “virtually everybody” who studies it agrees are strong claims, but the transcript supplies no sources, no counterarguments, and no distinction between consensus, common scholarly preference, and plausible reconstruction. The LDS film may indeed depict a better model, but a better model still remains a model unless anchored in public evidence. The messenger is not decisive; neither is Koukl’s confidence.

5. Elizabeth’s pregnancy as public corroboration

A narrative confirmation is not public proof of a virgin conception

Koukl’s apologetic observation is clever: Mary’s angelic encounter is private, but Elizabeth’s pregnancy could function as a checkable detail. If Mary was told something she could not have known, and if she then verified it, that would give her some reason within the story to trust the angelic message. Charitably, Koukl is not saying that a private feeling alone should ground belief; he is trying to identify a public-facing corroborative element in Luke’s infancy narrative.

But the move is still overextended. Modern listeners do not possess third-person public access to Elizabeth’s pregnancy; they possess a text reporting that Mary was told and then visited. Even if Elizabeth’s pregnancy were verified, that would not by itself verify an angel, the Holy Spirit’s causal role, or Jesus as divine Messiah. Mary’s surrender may be meaningful as a relational act within the story, but as epistemology, trusting a private revelation remains defective unless proportioned to independent evidence.

Overall Assessment

What survives, and what must be downgraded

What survives charitably is real but limited. Koukl gives the caller a plausible answer: “firstborn” need not mean “first created,” and Colossians 1:16 creates serious pressure against a creaturely reading if one accepts the text’s scope and authority. His willingness to correct sentimental Christmas traditions and to accept a useful depiction from an LDS production also shows a commendable source-neutral instinct.

What must fall is the confidence level. The episode repeatedly turns plausible internal readings into settled conclusions without supplying the lexical, historical, and public evidential controls required. The divinity inference is strong as Christian canonical reasoning but weak as public proof. The film recommendations may be pastorally useful, but emotional resonance is not evidence. The Elizabeth argument is the most inflated: a reported public detail inside a text is not public proof for us now.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality is darker than the episode admits: internal coherence is not public warrant, reverent storytelling is not historical confirmation, and a private revelation does not become evidence merely because the narrative attaches a checkable detail to it. The apologetic posture here keeps borrowing the emotional force of Christmas while quietly relaxing the standards that would be demanded of rival miracle traditions.

Faith, when used as a basis for confidence beyond the evidence, is not a virtue in this analysis; it is the defect being disguised. Mary’s surrender may be beautiful as a relational act within the story, and Christmas films may serve communal meaning, but neither supplies rational warrant for the truth of incarnation claims. If belief is to be rational, confidence must track evidence rather than devotion, identity, or seasonal warmth.

  • The prototokos argument survives as a plausible exegetical rebuttal, not as a decisive demonstration of Trinitarian ontology.
  • The uncreated-creator argument is valid only inside granted textual and theological premises; it does not independently establish the reality of a divine Christ.
  • The nativity-history claims require citations, lexical controls, and archaeological support before they can replace popular tradition with high confidence.

The challenge

The challenge: prove the leap from sacred story to public warrant, or retract the proof-talk

The weakest move in the episode is the inflation of internal narrative and devotional coherence into proof. When Koukl says “Elizabeth’s pregnancy is third person public,” the modern listener still has only the text’s report. That is not public access; it is a story about public access. If the episode wants to call this “proof to everybody,” it must produce independent controls: prior announcement, Mary’s ignorance, Elizabeth’s verified condition, and a reason that one confirmed ordinary pregnancy verifies a supernatural conception. Without that, the claim should be retracted.

The same pressure applies to “He's the uncreated creator” and “there were no inns in Bethlehem.” The first may be a valid conclusion for someone already granting Colossians and John as reliable divine testimony; it is not a public demonstration of metaphysics. The second may be a good historical correction; it is not established by saying “The Greek word is kataluma” and gesturing at what virtually everyone supposedly acknowledges. This episode must stop cashing out plausibility as certainty. Prove the lexical, historical, and evidential premises, or lower the rhetoric.

  • On “it means the preeminent one”: provide a full lexical survey and explain why the status sense wins in Colossians 1:15, not merely why it is theologically convenient.
  • On “by him, all things were created”: show why outsiders should treat these texts as reliable access to divine ontology, or admit the argument is internal Christian reasoning only.
  • On “Elizabeth's pregnancy is third person public”: distinguish public evidence available to Mary inside the story from public evidence available to us, or retract the claim that it proves the virgin conception.
