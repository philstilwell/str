A Three-Point Response to the Problem of Evil

Episode source
Stand to Reason Weekly Podcast / Podbean
Speaker

Transcript source for this critique

Greg Koukl’s three-point answer to the problem of evil has one real strength: it correctly refuses to let critics treat suffering as a merely tactical objection. But the episode repeatedly converts that fair point into overreach, especially when it equates ethical condemnation with objective divine law and treats unknown divine reasons as enough to blunt the force of actual suffering.

Critique Framework

How this page evaluates the episode

Calibration

Belief should track evidential support rather than identity, comfort, group pressure, or epistemic faith; trust or loyalty is not evidence for truth.

Symmetry

Inductive permission granted to Christianity must be granted to parallel claims unless a real differentiator is supplied.

Architecture

A source of conduct rules is not yet a public normative framework with access, binding force, scope, and repair.

Alternatives

Christian explanations must compete with secular, pluralist, psychological, and social explanations.

Bounded Agency

Concern should become proportionate action where agency exists, not inflated cosmic responsibility.

1. Evil as everyone’s problem

A fair burden-sharing move becomes a burden-shifting move

Koukl is strongest when he says suffering is not a puzzle Christians alone have to notice. Any serious worldview, religious or nonreligious, must say something intelligible about rape, murder, cruelty, grief, and social repair. That charitable reconstruction matters: he is not simply dodging the problem; he is asking whether the critic has a coherent account of the world once theism is removed. As a conversational tactic, that can prevent a shallow exchange where Christianity is interrogated and every alternative is left conveniently vague.

The repair would be to separate two questions Koukl blends: why suffering occurs, and why humans call certain conduct evil. Naturalistic accounts can address the first through biology, psychology, scarcity, social conflict, and chance without pretending that this removes pain. Moral non-realism can address the second without saying cruelty is pleasant, acceptable, or socially harmless. It only denies that ethical condemnation requires an ontological object called evil. Koukl’s challenge is legitimate against a critic who asserts objective evil while rejecting every possible ground for it; it is not decisive against non-realists who refuse that metaphysical vocabulary from the start.

Research anchors Does the absurdity of a universe with no ultimate justice make a God of ultimate justice… Ethical Anti-Realism The ultimate-justice analysis bears directly on Koukl’s move because it warns against inferring a divine ethical order from the emotional or practical intolerability of injustice. The Ethical Anti-Realism material supplies the missing distinction in the episode: denying objective stance-independent norm claims is not the same as denying harm, outrage, legal judgment, trauma, or cooperative norms. The local ethical-system threshold framework is relevant because Koukl names a source of divine command norm before demonstrating the architecture required for meaning, authority, access, correction, and disagreement.

The formalization shows why the opening move is partly successful but overextended. Suffering is shared evidence; the extra claim that it must be explained as objective evil is not shared unless the critic has already accepted a ethical-realist premise.

The episode pressures the critic toward two options: accept God as a solution or deny evil as unreal. That misses a third route: reject stance-independent stance-independent norm claims while preserving condemnation, harm assessment, social repair, and public norms.

Confirmation bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

Koukl interprets the critic’s outrage as latent confirmation of theism, rather than first asking whether the critic is using ethical language realistically, expressively, pragmatically, legally, or socially.

2. Possible divine reasons

Logical possibility is not evidential rescue

Koukl’s second Lego targets the deductive version of the problem of evil. Charitably, he is saying that the strict contradiction fails if it is even possible that a good and powerful God has ethically sufficient reasons to permit some evil temporarily. That is a meaningful distinction. If an argument claims logical impossibility, a coherent possible exception can weaken that specific deductive form. Koukl also correctly distinguishes the deductive issue from the later probabilistic issue, which is where much of the contemporary force actually lies.

The key epistemic repair is to mark the conclusion down. Koukl may say, “The deductive contradiction has not been established.” He may not responsibly imply, “The problem has been answered.” An unknown possible reason is not a reason in hand. It is an empty slot where a reason would have to go. Pastoral usefulness may comfort a suffering believer, but public warrant requires more: a non-ad hoc account that predicts or at least illuminates why this world, with these horrors, is what a perfectly good and powerful God would permit.

Research anchors God has a Plan Unfalsifiability The analysis of “God has a plan” bears on this exact inference because Koukl relies on a possible hidden divine rationale without specifying how that rationale is detected, constrained, or tested. The unfalsifiability material is also directly relevant: if every amount and kind of suffering can be absorbed by saying God might have reasons, the explanation stops making risky predictions. The belief-overreach framework helps identify where confidence outruns the actual support supplied.

Formalization

The possible-reason move works only against a universal premise; it does not establish that the Christian explanation is probable.

\[ \begin{aligned} P_1 &: \text{If God is good, God removes every evil immediately} \\ P_2 &: \text{Evil exists} \\ D &: \text{Therefore, no good and powerful God exists} \\ R &: \Diamond\,\text{God has a morally sufficient reason to permit some evil} \\ R &\Rightarrow \neg P_1 \text{ as a necessary universal} \\ R &\nRightarrow \Pr(\text{Christian God} \mid \text{actual evils}) \text{ is high}\end{aligned} \]

Assessment

The formalization grants Koukl the limited logical point: a universal premise can be defeated by a possible exception. But it also shows the illicit upgrade: possible reasons do not raise the probability of Christian theism unless they are specified, constrained, and compared with alternatives.

Hasty generalization Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The episode moves too quickly from defeating one deductive formulation to suggesting that the major intellectual pressure has been relieved. That generalizes beyond what the modal point earns.

Illusion of explanatory depth Diagnostic fit: High

The phrase “God might have a good reason” feels explanatory, but it contains no mechanism, no identifiable reason, no evidential markers, and no way to distinguish true providence from post hoc insulation.

3. Needles and greater goods

The vaccine analogy cannot carry the weight of horrendous evil

Koukl’s needle example is rhetorically effective because humans do sometimes permit short-term pain for a greater good. Parents authorize injections, dentists numb gums with unpleasant needles, and painful treatments can be justified by known benefits. The steelman is clear: if finite human caregivers can permit pain without losing their goodness, then divine permission of some pain is not automatically inconsistent with divine goodness.

The episode needs a bridge from “some permitted pain can be justified” to “this world’s full range of suffering is plausibly justified by a good God.” Without that bridge, the analogy trades on a halo around God’s presumed character. It asks listeners to import the trust they have in a parent or dentist into a cosmic context where the alleged agent, methods, reasons, and outcomes are hidden. Relational trust is meaningful after a relationship is independently established; it is not evidence that the metaphysical claim is true.

The formalization isolates the analogical load. The transcript establishes that some pain can be justified, but it does not show that the relevant structure of medical care maps onto apparently gratuitous suffering, natural disasters, abuse, or prolonged horror.

False analogy Diagnostic fit: High

A child’s vaccination and the world’s severe suffering differ in too many ethically and evidentially relevant respects for the former to substantially justify the latter.

Halo effect Diagnostic fit: Moderate

Because God is already assumed to be good, the analogy receives charitable assumptions that would not be granted to an unknown powerful agent permitting comparable harms without explanation.

4. No God, no evil

The ethical argument trades on an equivocation about evil

Koukl’s third Lego is the most ambitious: if there is no God, there is no evil. The steelman is that he is targeting critics who use evil as an objective feature of reality while rejecting the kind of transcendent lawgiver he thinks allegedly objective conduct norms requires. If someone insists that evil is a stance-independent violation of binding divine command norm, then it is fair to ask what grounds that law and why it has authority over everyone.

The speed-limit analogy makes the problem vivid but not decisive. Human speed limits are enacted by governments because they are legal conventions. That does not show all normativity requires a cosmic governor; it shows that legal rules require institutions. Human communities also generate norms from vulnerability, reciprocity, emotional aversion, shared interests, and negotiated public reasons. Koukl still owes the hard part: why divine commands or God’s nature explain normative authority better than human norm-making, and how humans reliably access that divine standard amid deep religious disagreement.

The formalization shows the hidden substitution. If objective evil is granted, the argument can begin. But ordinary ethical language supplies human condemnation, not necessarily the objectivist premise. The conclusion depends on what “evil” means.

Equivocation Diagnostic fit: High

Confirmation bias Diagnostic fit: High

Koukl treats people’s ethical reactions as evidence for the exact theistic ethical ontology he already accepts, while discounting non-realist explanations of those same reactions.

5. Humanly touched Scripture

A good self-defeat critique followed by an underdeveloped Jesus pivot

In the caller segment, Koukl gives one of the episode’s better pieces of reasoning. The nephew’s claim that anything touched by humans is corrupt is self-undermining if taken globally. Human feelings, college learning, spoken claims, written mathematics, and the statement itself are all humanly mediated. Koukl is right that a blanket genetic dismissal cannot bear its own weight. The source of a claim can raise questions about reliability, but it does not automatically settle truth value.

Koukl’s pivot to Jesus of Nazareth is strategically sensible, because Christian apologetics does not need every disputed biblical episode to be settled before discussing the core Christian claim. But the pivot must not become an evidential shortcut. The life, conduct, death, and alleged resurrection of Jesus are still accessed through human testimony and theological texts. If faith means relational trust after sufficient evidence, it belongs downstream of the historical case. If faith functions as confidence beyond the evidence, it is an epistemic defect, not a virtue.

Selection bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

Overall Assessment

What survives, and what must be marked down

The episode survives best where it makes modest distinctions. Koukl is right that suffering is a universal human concern, that a crude deductive problem of evil needs a stronger universal premise than many popular versions provide, and that the caller’s blanket rejection of humanly mediated Scripture is self-defeating. Those are real points, not mere evasions.

The confidence level must fall wherever those modest points are inflated. A possible hidden reason is not an explanation. A vaccine analogy is not a theodicy for horrendous suffering. Ordinary ethical condemnation is not already objective divine law. And refuting “humans touched the Bible” does not establish the resurrection or biblical reliability. The episode repeatedly wins a smaller point and then speaks as though it has won a larger one.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality is darker than the episode allows: the God hypothesis is doing less explanatory work than the rhetoric suggests. “God might have a reason” can be made compatible with any amount of suffering, which means it risks explaining nothing in particular. An explanation that survives every possible observation by becoming more hidden, more inscrutable, and more elastic is not gaining rational credibility; it is avoiding exposure.

Faith, where it means relational loyalty after independent warrant, is psychologically intelligible. Faith, where it means confidence beyond the evidence, is irrational. The nephew’s “feeling in my heart” is not a public warrant, but neither is the Christian’s untested confidence that all evils fit an unseen providential plan. Both must be disciplined by evidence-proportioned belief.

  • Pastoral comfort may help a suffering believer endure, but it does not publicly justify the claim that a good, powerful God permits evil for ethically sufficient reasons.
  • ethical outrage does not automatically reveal objective ethical furniture; it can arise from harm recognition, empathy, social dependence, fear, solidarity, and negotiated norms.
  • The strict deductive problem may be weakened, but the probabilistic and character-based problems remain alive and require much more than modal possibility.

The challenge

The challenge — Stop calling hidden permission an explanation

Dark red challenge: the weakest move in this transcript is the combination of “is it possible that God might have a good reason” with the sweeping slogan “if there is no God, then there is no evil.” That is not a solution to suffering; it is a pincer maneuver. When evil threatens God, evil is absorbed into unknown divine reasons. When evil threatens atheism, evil is treated as objective law that only God can ground. The episode must prove that switch is principled, not opportunistic, or retract the confidence attached to it.

The needle analogy makes the problem worse, not better, if it is asked to carry the real burden. “I know that this evil will result in a better good” works for a parent authorizing a shot because the mechanism, scale, purpose, and outcome are visible. Koukl has not shown the equivalent for the world’s worst suffering. If the episode wants to claim that divine permission is ethically comparable to medical care, it must identify the relevant similarities and answer the disanalogies. If it cannot, the analogy should be retired rather than used to soften horrors it has not explained.

  • The claim “if there is no God, then there is no evil” must prove that human condemnation, harm-based norms, legal accountability, and public ethical reasoning are meaningless without divine law, or retract the slogan.
  • The possible-reason defense must specify constraints on what God would not permit; otherwise it is compatible with everything and evidentially confirms nothing.
  • The Jesus pivot after “everything touched by man” must provide positive historical evidence under symmetrical standards, or admit that defeating a bad objection to Scripture is not yet an argument for Christianity.
