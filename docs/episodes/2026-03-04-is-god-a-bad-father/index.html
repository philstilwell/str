Is God a Bad Father?

Episode source
Stand to Reason Weekly Podcast / Podbean
Speaker

This episode tries to answer the charge that the biblical God is a bad father by narrowing divine fatherhood, widening God’s authority roles, and then relocating nonbelievers outside the father-child relation altogether. The later call extends the same theological pattern into sanctification: hardship, discipline, rewards, and differing degrees of heavenly glory are treated as a coherent answer to why earthly formation matters if deathbed converts can be glorified immediately.

1. Divine fatherhood narrowed by competing metaphors

The father metaphor is protected by weakening it exactly when it becomes ethically costly

Steelmanned, Greg’s answer is that critics are flattening biblical language. Scripture does not present God only as a parent; it also speaks of God as master, sovereign, and potter. On that reading, the charge that God is a bad father may overextend one metaphor beyond its intended range. A father-child analogy can illuminate care, intimacy, inheritance, discipline, and dependence without implying that every divine action must resemble ideal human parenting. Greg’s strongest point is hermeneutical: if the Bible itself gives multiple models for the God-human relationship, then critics should not isolate the warmest one and make it govern every case.

The critique is not that analogical language is illegitimate. Human language is often analogical, and religious language especially so. The problem is that Greg uses analogy management as a defense against an ethical challenge without showing that the resulting picture remains worthy of trust. From a moral non-realist standpoint, the issue is not whether there is an objective ethical object called goodness floating beyond human discourse. The issue is whether the community’s own words of love, care, justice, mercy, and fatherhood remain socially and semantically coherent when attached to actions that, in ordinary human contexts, would be condemned as harmful, coercive, or abusive. Multiplying metaphors may explain the text; it does not by itself vindicate the character described by the text.

Motivated reasoning Diagnostic fit: High

The interpretive system preserves the desired conclusion that God remains good by adjusting which metaphor controls the case. The difficult evidence is not allowed to discipline the model; the model reclassifies the evidence.

2. Creator ownership converted into punitive authority

Making humans does not automatically justify ruling, punishing, or silencing them

Steelmanned, Greg is articulating a classic Christian sovereignty claim: if God created everything, then everything belongs to God, and creaturely complaint must be situated under divine authority. The potter-clay and king-domain images are meant to remind listeners that humans are not God’s peers. On this view, the critic’s question is not just ethically mistaken but metaphysically mispositioned: a made being is challenging the maker as if the maker were another citizen under the same jurisdiction. That argument has internal coherence inside a worldview where creation establishes authority and authority includes judgment.

From outside the Christian premise set, this is not a solution to the bad-father charge; it is a shift from care to power. Saying God made humans may explain why God can affect humans, but it does not show that every divine use of power is admirable, fair, loving, or worth obeying. A moral non-realist critique can still ask what social and emotional work the words justice, rebellion, and authority are doing. If they function mainly to end the conversation by relocating God above critique, then they are not public reasons. They are insider status markers. The episode needs to show why the creator-creature relation licenses the specific disputed acts rather than simply making dissent sound impudent.

Authority bias Diagnostic fit: High

3. Outsiders classified as rebels rather than children

The insider-outsider move turns unbelief into rebellion before proving the war exists

Steelmanned, Greg is distinguishing universal creation from covenant membership. On his reading, God creates all people, but Scripture reserves the intimate child-of-God status for those who receive Christ. That distinction is textually important within many Christian systems, and it helps explain why some biblical promises are not applied indiscriminately to everyone. Greg is also trying to avoid sentimental universal fatherhood that would make judgment passages incoherent. The strongest form of the claim is not that Christians are naturally better, but that Christ changes their standing before God.

The sharper problem is ethical and epistemic. If a sincere non-Christian has not been given adequate evidence, then calling that person a rebel is not an explanation; it is a verdict in advance of the trial. Biblical faith-language can mean loyalty, reliance, or trust within an already accepted relationship. But when faith in Christ is used as the boundary marker that determines whether God is angry or fatherly, the claim needs evidential support. Faith-based confidence that one’s group is at peace with God while outsiders are under wrath is not an epistemic virtue. It is a risky overbelief unless it tracks evidence available beyond the group’s own texts.

False dilemma Diagnostic fit: High

Ingroup bias Diagnostic fit: High

The episode assigns peace, family, and divine favor to the in-group while characterizing outsiders as rebels whose prayers may be abominable. The evidential asymmetry is built into the social boundary.

4. Unequal heavenly glory as the purpose of sanctification

The reward hierarchy answers the caller by multiplying unverified afterlife mechanics

Steelmanned, Greg gives Katie a real answer to a real puzzle. If a deathbed convert and a lifelong disciple are both saved, why does sanctification matter? His answer is that salvation is not the only relevant category. Earthly sanctification forms a person for a richer participation in the life to come, and Scripture suggests differing rewards, better resurrection, and works that survive judgment. That preserves grace while still making earthly faithfulness consequential. It also avoids the crude idea that sanctification earns salvation; Greg explicitly distinguishes merit for rewards from merit for being saved.

The pastoral value is obvious: the doctrine motivates perseverance and gives meaning to long obedience. But pastoral usefulness is not evidence. A Mormon afterlife map, a Catholic purgatorial map, and Greg’s Protestant reward hierarchy can all answer the caller’s practical question in internally coherent ways. The rational task is not to ask which story feels most spiritually productive, but which has evidential support proportionate to its specificity. Here Greg’s confidence should be modest. He offers proof texts and metaphors, not independent access to postmortem conditions. Faith as trust within a Christian life may motivate practice; faith as confidence that these unseen reward mechanics are real exceeds the evidence supplied.

5. Hardship recoded as glory-production

Suffering becomes meaningful by doctrine, not by demonstrated divine design

Steelmanned, Greg’s suffering-and-glory answer is pastorally serious. He does not say discipline is pleasant. He explicitly acknowledges that walking with Christ is hard and that discipline feels sorrowful in the moment. His claim is that God redeems hardship by forming godliness, and that earthly affliction produces an eternal weight of glory. This gives believers a way to endure pain without treating it as meaningless. In its most charitable form, the claim is not that every pain is obviously good, but that God can use hardship in the sanctification process.

The ethical problem is not solved by saying that hardship later produces glory. In ordinary human normative discourse, intentionally allowing preventable suffering requires serious justification, especially if the agent is described as loving and powerful. If the answer is deferred to heaven, the claim becomes unavailable for public assessment now. The academic source on overextended responsibility is relevant here because religious interpretations can expand perceived burden: suffering people may be taught not only to endure pain but to interpret it as a test, opportunity, or failure of response. That can help some people cope, but it can also sanctify avoidable harm. Pastoral usefulness and metaphysical truth have to be separated.

Overall Assessment

What survives is textual coherence; what fails is public justification

Charitably, the episode does several things well. It notices that biblical God-language is multifaceted rather than merely sentimental. It distinguishes covenant adoption from generic creation. It tries to preserve grace while explaining why sanctification matters. It also avoids promising that discipline feels good or that late converts are unsaved. Those are real internal clarifications within Greg’s Christian framework.

But the confidence levels must fall sharply. The episode does not establish that God exists, that the Bible reliably reveals God’s character, that creator-status entails ethically legitimate authority over persons, that outsiders are culpable rebels, that their prayers are abominable, that heavenly glory varies by sanctification, or that hardship produces eternal glory. Most of the argument is doctrinal arrangement, not evidence responsive reasoning. It may tell believers how to organize inherited texts; it does not give critics a public reason to stop asking whether the character being described is worthy of trust.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality: the theology explains itself by moving the burden out of reach

The epistemic reality is darker than the episode admits. When fatherhood becomes too ethically demanding, Greg retreats to sovereignty. When sovereignty sounds cold, he returns to intimacy for insiders. When outsiders make the fatherhood objection, they are reclassified as rebels. When suffering looks gratuitous, it becomes discipline. When sanctification seems unnecessary, unseen degrees of glory are introduced. Every pressure point is answered by another internal theological lever, but none of those levers is independently anchored.

This is how a belief system can feel deep while remaining under-tested. The claims are not meaningless, and they are not insincere. They are worse than that epistemically: they are flexible enough to protect confidence without earning it. Faith, when it functions as confidence beyond the evidence, is not a virtue here. Trust and loyalty may have relational meaning after a relationship is established, but they cannot supply the warrant for believing that the relationship, the wrath, the rewards, and the hidden glory-economy are real.

  • The father defense survives only as a limited hermeneutical point: biblical metaphors differ. It does not show that the God-character is loving, just, or publicly defensible.
  • The insider-outsider claim must be downgraded from description of humanity to internal Christian classification unless independent evidence shows that non-Christian unbelief is culpable rebellion.
  • The sanctification and suffering claims should be held as speculative pastoral theology, not as knowledge of postmortem reality or divine intention.

The challenge

The challenge: prove the sovereignty escape or retract the Father defense

The weakest point in the transcript is the role-switching around God’s character. Greg says, ‘He is a father, kind of, sort of,’ and warns that critics err ‘if we press the metaphor too far.’ But the episode itself presses fatherhood when it wants warmth and then evacuates fatherhood when the critic points to hell, wrath, or biblical judgment. That is not a sound answer to the bad-father charge. It is a controlled demolition of the word father. If fatherhood has no stable constraint on how God treats vulnerable creatures, then stop using the term as though it carries the ethical tenderness of parenting. If it does have such constraint, then show how eternal wrath, outsider exclusion, and punitive divine rule satisfy it.

The second failure is the authoritarian shortcut: ‘God's in charge. He made it. It's his.’ That line must prove more than power. It must prove legitimacy. It must prove that making conscious beings gives the maker the right to classify them as rebels, refuse their prayers, threaten them, discipline them through suffering, and then call the whole system love. And the final section doubles the problem: ‘The hardships produce the glory’ is asserted while the mechanism remains hidden and the evidence remains internal. The episode must either demonstrate, with criteria that could in principle fail, that these are real divine arrangements, or retract the confidence with which it presents them as answers.

  • The father metaphor cannot be devotional gold and critical vapor. The episode must specify when fatherhood constrains God’s conduct or admit that the word is being used asymmetrically.
  • The outsider category cannot be imposed before the evidential case is made. The claim that non-Christians are rebels whose prayers may be abominable must be proved, not merely read from insider texts.
  • The glory economy cannot rescue sanctification by speculation. If ‘Some get more glory than the others’ is more than motivational imagery, the episode must provide a disciplined evidential model or lower the claim to conjecture.

Calibration Tests

Evidence that would change the assessment

The transcript supplies mostly internal theological reasoning: biblical metaphors, proof texts, analogies, and pastoral illustrations. It does not supply independent evidence that God exists, that the Bible accurately represents God, that divine anger is real, that prayers are refused by God, or that postmortem glory varies by earthly sanctification. Those omissions matter because the episode often moves from textual interpretation to claims about reality.
