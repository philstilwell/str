Why an Emotivist Can’t Use Suffering to Argue Against the Existence of God

This episode’s central apologetic move is to turn suffering back against the atheist: if Alex O’Connor is an emotivist, Greg argues, then suffering cannot function as a real objection to God. The stronger charitable reading is that Greg is asking whether a critic may use ethical language while denying stance-independent stance-independent norm claims. The critique is that he repeatedly treats that metaethical question as if it defeats the evidential, pastoral, and public-warrant force of suffering itself.

1. Emotivism and the suffering objection

A non-realist can still press an internal evidential problem

Steelmanned, Greg’s point is not trivial. If someone says suffering is objectively evil while also denying objective stance-independent norm claims, there is a tension to clarify. A critic who uses words like evil, wicked, or ought must explain whether those words mean stance-independent properties, emotional condemnation, human welfare judgments, social-contract violations, or an internal critique of Christian claims. In that narrow sense, Greg is right to ask Alex O’Connor what kind of ethical language he is using.

From a moral non-realist perspective, the critique of God need not be: “there exists an ontological object called evil, therefore God is impossible.” It can be: “Christianity describes God as loving, providential, and able to prevent severe suffering; the world contains vast apparently unredeemed suffering; therefore the Christian model loses evidential fit unless it supplies a better explanation.” Greg answers a metaethical version of the problem, but the stronger evidential version survives.

Research anchors Ethical Anti-Realism Borrowing from Christianity? The moral anti-realist anchor bears directly on this inference because it separates ethical language from stance-independent ethical furniture while preserving human condemnation, harm reasoning, and public justification. The borrowing-style critique is relevant because Greg’s objection resembles the claim that secular critics must secretly rely on Christian ethical metaphysics before they can complain about suffering.

Premise 2 is the failure point. A critic can use suffering as an internal disconfirmation of Christian theism without affirming stance-independent stance-independent norm claims. 2. Animal pain as designed trade-off

The engineering analogy explains warning pain, not the world’s suffering profile

Steelmanned, Greg’s point about pain has real explanatory value at the biological level. Pain often functions as a warning signal, and any embodied organism with fragile tissues needs feedback that motivates avoidance, rest, protection, and repair. The “mind the gap” analogy is rhetorically effective because some dangers accompany otherwise useful systems. A world with sensation may be better, in many cases, than a world of numb organisms self-destructing unnoticed.

The analogy also imports human engineering constraints into a debate about an omnipotent designer. A subway needs a gap because rails, platforms, and moving carriages impose physical limitations. But a classical God is not supposed to be a municipal engineer trapped by trade-offs. If the claim is that even God must use these particular biological mechanisms, that constraint needs argument. If God need not use them, then the analogy lowers the cost of suffering only rhetorically, not evidentially.

Formalization

The argument can be stated as an inference from biological function to theodicy.

\[ \begin{aligned} &1.\ \text{Pain often has protective biological function.} \\ &2.\ \text{Valuable designed systems often include trade-offs.} \\ &3.\ \text{Animal suffering results from the trade-off required by protective pain.} \\ &\therefore\ \text{animal suffering is compatible with good divine design.} \end{aligned} \]

Assessment

The conclusion is possible but much weaker than Greg presents. Premise 1 is plausible; premise 2 is plausible for finite designers; premise 3 is too broad and unproved. Compatibility is not probability, and a design label does not explain excess suffering.

False analogy Diagnostic fit: High

The subway-gap analogy compares finite human engineering constraints with divine creation. That comparison hides the very issue in dispute: whether an all-powerful designer needed such harmful biological trade-offs.

Illusion of explanatory depth Diagnostic fit: High

The explanation feels complete because pain has an obvious function, but the account does not specify why this amount, distribution, duration, and variety of suffering would be chosen by a loving omnipotent God.

3. The Fall and inherited consequences

Causal ripple is not the same as justified cosmic penalty

Steelmanned, Greg’s analogy to Hitler and Pearl Harbor identifies something real: decisions by a few people can impose horrific consequences on many others who did not choose them. Human life is causally entangled. Political leaders, parents, inventors, soldiers, and institutions can create conditions inherited by later people. So the mere idea that one action affects many is not absurd.

In a moral non-realist frame, the objection is not that a cosmic divine command norm has been violated by inherited suffering. The objection is public and structural: a system that assigns catastrophic consequences to unconsulted descendants because of an ancient act looks unjustifiable by ordinary standards of agency, consent, proportionality, and harm reduction. Calling those consequences “sin” does not repair the inference; it redescribes the mechanism in insider theological language.

Motivated reasoning Diagnostic fit: Moderate

4. Evil as evidence for God

Comfort is not warrant, and outrage is not ethical ontology

Steelmanned, Greg is pressing a familiar ethical argument: if someone calls suffering evil in an objective sense, then that language seems to require more than blind nature. Christianity can tell a story in which evil is real, suffering is not final, and redemption eventually answers tragedy. That story may be pastorally meaningful to believers, especially when grief would otherwise feel abandoned to chance.

Greg also overstates the atheist position when he says it has nothing to offer the suffering mother. It may have no promise of resurrection, but it can offer presence, medicine, honesty, shared grief, advocacy, and refusal to explain the child’s cancer as downstream from ancestral sin. The Christian answer may comfort some listeners, but it also adds claims that require proof: Fall, redemption, divine permission, future restoration, and the goodness of the plan.

5. Autism, fallen creation, and soul-making

Pastoral hope cannot justify instrumentalized suffering

Steelmanned, Greg is trying to respond pastorally to a painful family question rather than merely win a debate. He does not mock the caller’s son. He acknowledges anguish, respects the son’s progress, and tries to locate disability within a larger Christian hope of resurrection and repair. He also distinguishes immediate emotional satisfaction from ultimate salvation, which is pastorally more careful than promising easy answers.

The episode also leaves divine hiddenness unresolved for people whose cognitive or emotional conditions make ordinary religious relationship difficult. If God desires relationship, then an all-knowing God should be able to accommodate neurodiversity, trauma, emotional flatness, psychopathy, autism, depression, or any other human variation without requiring guesswork. Saying some things wait until resurrection may be existentially useful, but as public warrant it functions like a promissory note that cannot be checked now.

Overall Assessment

What survives charitably, and what confidence must fall

What survives charitably is limited but real. Greg is right that critics should clarify their metaethics, that some pain has protective function, that human choices can affect later innocents, that Christian hope can comfort believers, and that the caller’s family deserved a humane pastoral response rather than a sterile debate answer.

What must fall is the confidence level attached to the apologetic conclusions. The episode does not prove that an emotivist cannot use suffering against God, does not show that animal suffering is explained by design trade-offs, does not justify inherited Fall consequences, does not establish evil as evidence for God, and does not warrant autism as a soul-making consequence of sin. These remain theological possibilities, not publicly established explanations.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality is dark: this episode repeatedly converts Christian interpretive resources into answers before those resources have earned public warrant. “The Fall,” “soul-making,” “God has a plan,” and “resurrection will fix it” may function as intra-Christian consolation, but consolation is not evidence. When faith fills the gap between anguish and explanation, it is not an epistemic virtue; it is belief outrunning support.

The bleakest feature is the asymmetry. Alex’s moral non-realism is scrutinized aggressively, but Christian claims about ancestral sin, cosmic brokenness, divine permission, and future restoration are allowed to operate with far less evidential pressure. If rational belief means credence proportioned to evidence, then the episode’s Christian confidence must be reduced to modest possibility unless the missing premises are defended.

  • Do not confuse moral non-realism with indifference. A person can reject objective stance-independent norm claims and still condemn suffering through harm, welfare, solidarity, preference, and public justification.
  • Do not confuse compatibility with explanation. Showing that Christianity has a story about suffering does not show that the story is likely, necessary, or better than rival explanations.
  • Do not confuse pastoral usefulness with truth. Resurrection hope, divine purpose, and soul-making may comfort believers, but they cannot be used as evidence unless independently warranted.

The challenge

The challenge: prove the hidden premises or retract the disqualification of the critic

The weakest move in the transcript is the attempt to knock Alex O’Connor out of court by saying he is “smuggling it in.” That is not a refutation of the suffering argument; it is a gatekeeping maneuver. If Greg wants to say an emotivist cannot object to suffering, he must prove that the only relevant objection requires objective stance-independent norm claims. Otherwise he must retract the claim. The critic can press a devastating internal question without borrowing Christian ethical discourse: why does a loving, powerful God’s world look like this?

The second major failure is the theological laundering of suffering through “Pain is a design feature,” “That’s the destructive nature of sin,” and “some things don’t ever change until the resurrection.” These phrases do not prove what they need to prove. They merely rename the cost. The episode must show that an omnipotent God needed these trade-offs, that descendants justly inherit Fall consequences, that disability is not being instrumentalized for others’ virtue, and that future repair is more than faith-based promissory comfort. If it cannot, the claims should be presented as pastoral speculation, not apologetic explanation.

  • On emotivism, answer the actual internal critique: why is observed suffering not strong evidence against the Christian profile of a loving, powerful, providential God?
  • On animal pain, prove that the full suffering profile is a necessary or best trade-off for an omnipotent designer, not merely that some pain has biological function.
  • On autism and the Fall, either justify inherited suffering and involuntary soul-making with public reasons, or stop presenting them as explanations rather than consoling theological guesses.

◉ Evidence-Proportionate Belief: Assess whether claims about resurrection, lordship, creation, Fall, redemption, and final restoration receive enough evidence to justify the confidence placed in them.
◉ Pastoral Usefulness Versus Truth: Evaluate whether the existential usefulness of hope, identity, and calling is being treated as evidence that the worldview is true.
◉ Narrative Closure: Analyze whether a promised ending functions as a circular story-ending premise that predetermines the interpretation of present events.
◉ Insider Authority: Examine whether appeals to Scripture and Christian tradition establish the claims only for insiders who already grant those sources authority.
◉ Resurrection Evidence Gap: Evaluate moves from mentioning evidence to treating resurrection as a central reality when the actual evidence is not presented.
◉ Lordship Claim Expansion: Assess whether lordship claims are argued as public facts or merely proclaimed as theological commitments.
◉ Equivocation Risk: Check for shifts in the meanings of worldview, hope, truth, Lord, good, fallen, redeemed, identity, and calling.
◉ Cultural-Moment Framing: Examine whether culture-war examples create a false dilemma, strawman, or asymmetric framing.
◉ Analogy Limits: Test whether literary or pastoral analogies legitimately support public conclusions.
◉ Historical Selectivity: Evaluate uses of church-history examples for cherry-picking, survivorship bias, halo effect, or hasty generalization.
◉ Normative-Claim Threshold: Ask whether theological categories generate determinate conduct guidance without additional contested premises.
◉ Inductive Symmetry: Compare the standards used to accept Christian explanatory claims with the standards required for rival worldviews.
◉ Scope Leakage: Identify moves from this helps Christian students live with hope and purpose to therefore Christianity is true or uniquely adequate.
◉ Burden of Proof and Special Pleading: Determine whether rival views are asked to justify themselves while Christian claims are exempted from comparable scrutiny.
◉ Non Sequitur Risk: Identify conclusions that do not follow from premises, especially from prosocial motivation to metaphysical truth or from scriptural narrative to public epistemic warrant.
