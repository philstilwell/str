What Does It Mean to Be Made in God’s Image?

Episode source
Stand to Reason Weekly Podcast / Podbean
Speaker

This episode is at its best when Koukl resists popular evangelical shortcuts: he protects human worth from ability-based ranking, rejects a crude use of Jeremiah 29:11, and demystifies the vague language of being called to ministry. The weak points appear when internal biblical reasoning is treated as if it supplies public warrant, when paradox is renamed mystery, and when pastoral plausibility is allowed to carry more confidence than the evidence supplied in the exchange.

Critique Framework

How this page evaluates the episode

Calibration

Belief should track evidential support rather than identity, comfort, group pressure, or epistemic faith; trust or loyalty is not evidence for truth.

Symmetry

Inductive permission granted to Christianity must be granted to parallel claims unless a real differentiator is supplied.

Architecture

A source of conduct rules is not yet a public normative framework with access, binding force, scope, and repair.

Alternatives

Christian explanations must compete with secular, pluralist, psychological, and social explanations.

Bounded Agency

Concern should become proportionate action where agency exists, not inflated cosmic responsibility.

1. Intrinsic image-of-God dignity

A humane doctrine is doing more work than the text can publicly support

Koukl’s strongest move in the image-of-God discussion is to reject a capacity-based account of human worth. He hears the caller’s worry correctly: if reason, creativity, or ethical performance is what makes someone an image bearer, then infants, disabled persons, dementia patients, and others with diminished capacities would seem to possess less worth. His alternative is better as pastoral ethics: the image is intrinsic, not earned by performance, and therefore no human being should be ranked by usefulness, intelligence, or visible ability.

The weakness is that Koukl moves from an internal biblical declaration to broad metaphysical and ethical conclusions: ultimate value, special obligations, and even the legitimacy of capital punishment. From a moral non-realist perspective, ethical language is human normative discourse: we coordinate, condemn, protect, praise, and negotiate around harm and welfare. Calling those practices grounded in the image of God adds a theological story, but the episode does not show that the story is true or that it explains human rights better than shared vulnerability and cooperative social life.

Authority bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

Scriptural authority carries much of the argumentative weight. That may persuade insiders, but it can make the conclusion feel established before the underlying source and interpretation have been independently assessed.

2. Kenosis and omniscience

Calling contradiction mystery does not make it coherent

Koukl gives a standard orthodox answer to the caller’s question about whether Jesus knew modern scientific facts during his earthly ministry. He distinguishes Jesus’ divine nature from his human nature, says Jesus did not empty himself of divinity, and argues that in his human life he lacked some knowledge. Charitably, this is an attempt to preserve both full divinity and genuine humanity while taking seriously texts where Jesus appears limited, grows, learns, or says he does not know something.

The critique is not that every unresolved theological question is automatically false. The critique is that Koukl’s confidence outruns the explanation supplied. Saying he would simply bear testimony to the text is honest as an insider posture, but it is not a public resolution of the logical pressure. Rival religions can also preserve contradictions by dividing respects, appealing to mystery, and citing sacred texts. A fair standard must ask whether the distinction predicts anything, clarifies anything, or merely prevents disconfirmation.

Begging the question Diagnostic fit: High

Motivated reasoning Diagnostic fit: High

The reasoning is motivated by the need to preserve both full divinity and textual limitations. The distinction may be defensible, but the episode does not test it with the same severity it would apply to non-Christian paradoxes.

3. Discipleship by modeling

Parental formation can explain retention without proving truth

The advice to the father is practically sensible: children are shaped by what parents repeatedly do, not merely by what parents formally teach. Shared meals, prayer, visible sincerity, age-appropriate conversations, and time spent together can form identity and habits. As pastoral counsel, this is largely unobjectionable. It is also healthier than outsourcing a child’s entire religious formation to programs, youth events, or apologetics curricula.

The unsupported part is the remembered study and the near-determinative framing. Koukl mentions an 85 percent figure but does not name the study, define the measured outcome, state controls, or distinguish sincere adult conviction from inherited identity. Even if the statistic is accurate, it would show transmission, not warrant. The very mechanisms that make discipleship effective can also produce authority bias, ingroup loyalty, and fear of disconfirmation unless children are taught evidential symmetry and genuine intellectual autonomy.

4. Jeremiah 29:11 in context

A good anti-prooftexting argument that should be applied more widely

Koukl’s answer on Jeremiah 29:11 is one of the episode’s strongest pieces of reasoning. He presses the context: if someone claims the welfare promise as a personal promise because all promises are yes in Christ, why not also claim the surrounding promises about restored fortunes, return from exile, or the later promises of sword, famine, and pestilence? This is a legitimate reductio against a selective devotional reading.

The overstatement comes when he calls the opposing use a distortion and corruption of God’s word. That may be fair against crude prooftexting, but the caller mentions more nuanced canonical arguments from 2 Corinthians and 1 Peter. Koukl answers the crude form decisively; he does not fully adjudicate every typological or canonical version. Still, his central warning is sound: devotional usefulness is not the same as authorial meaning, and emotional attachment to a verse does not create an interpretive right to claim it.

The argument succeeds against an indiscriminate promise-claiming hermeneutic. It does not automatically defeat every canonical theology of fulfillment, but it forces that theology to specify criteria rather than cherry-pick comforting verses.

False dilemma Diagnostic fit: Moderate

Koukl’s strongest language can make it sound as though the options are his contextual reading or corruption, when there may be more nuanced canonical readings that still require evaluation.

Confirmation bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The episode rightly challenges a popular evangelical reading, but it does not ask whether the same context-first method would destabilize other Christian prooftexts that Koukl favors.

5. Calling to ministry

Demystifying calling helps, but supernatural gifting still needs evidence

Koukl gives a refreshing answer to the caller worried about whether he has been called to apologetics ministry. He challenges the received evangelical tradition that a subjective sense of calling is required, asks where Scripture makes such a feeling necessary, and shifts attention to gifts, desire, fruit, and capability. This is one of the episode’s more rationally healthy moments because it relieves a person from trying to decode vague inner signals as if they were divine instructions.

The remaining problem is that gifting is treated as the distribution mechanism without evidence that the gifts are supernatural rather than ordinary talents, interests, temperaments, and learned skills. Koukl’s own story can be explained without invoking divine assignment: he liked apologetics, had teaching ability, practiced for decades, and found a niche. That is enough. Adding the Holy Spirit may be meaningful within Christian identity, but it is not established by the vocational facts he cites.

Overall Assessment

What survives and what must be downgraded

The charitable remainder is real. Koukl’s intrinsic-image answer protects vulnerable humans from ability-based ranking. His Jeremiah answer is a strong rebuke to devotional prooftexting. His ministry-calling answer wisely reduces the burden of decoding vague inner impressions. These are not trivial strengths; they show a better apologetic instinct than simplistic slogan-based Christianity.

The downgraded confidence is also real. The episode does not publicly establish that humans bear a divine image, that asserted obligations are grounded in God, that the incarnation is coherent rather than merely traditional, that parental discipleship statistics support a near-determinative claim, or that ministry gifts are supernatural. Internal Christian coherence, pastoral usefulness, and sincere testimony are not the same as public warrant.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality: faith cannot rescue overconfidence

The dark epistemic reality is that several claims in the episode are protected by insider assumptions rather than earned by evidence. Scripture is repeatedly used as a load-bearing authority, but the truth of the scriptural framework is not argued in the relevant places. When the topic is Jeremiah, Koukl demands context and consistency. When the topic is incarnation, image-bearing, or supernatural gifting, the standards become more permissive.

Faith, when it functions as confidence beyond evidence, is not an epistemic virtue. Biblical faith-language can mean trust, reliance, allegiance, or loyalty, but none of those relational postures establishes that a proposition is true. If a Christian trusts God because Christianity is already warranted, that is one thing. If trust is used to keep confidence high when the warrant is missing, that is irrational overbelief.

  • Pastoral value survives: protecting the vulnerable, resisting prooftexting, and lowering anxiety about calling are useful outcomes even when the metaphysics remains unproved.
  • Public warrant falls: Genesis, kenosis, Holy Spirit gifting, and remembered retention statistics cannot carry the level of confidence sometimes implied in the episode.
  • Symmetry is mandatory: the contextual rigor used against Jeremiah 29:11 prooftexting must also be applied to Christian uses of Hebrew Bible prophecy, divine promises, and doctrinal harmonization.

The challenge

The challenge: stop condemning prooftexting in one room while hiding mystery in another

The weakest move in the transcript is not the Jeremiah correction; that is one of the strongest. The weakest move is the selective courage. Koukl can see immediately that ‘why doesn’t it apply to verse 14’ exposes an illegitimate promise grab. Good. Now apply that same acid to the rest of the episode. If context can puncture a beloved welfare verse, then context can also puncture inflated image-of-God claims, harmonized christological tensions, and convenient appeals to supernatural gifting. The episode must either prove that its favored doctrines survive the same contextual and evidential pressure, or retract the implied certainty.

The Jesus answer is especially exposed. Saying ‘in Jesus’ human nature, he was not omniscient’ and then retreating to ‘I would just bear testimony to the things’ is not an explanation; it is a doctrinal ceasefire. Rival religions can play that game forever. The ministry answer shows Koukl knows better: he rejects vague calling language because no one can define it and no verse requires it. That same discipline should be turned on mystery, image-bearing, and Holy Spirit gifting. Define the mechanism, show the evidence, or lower the claim.

  • The image-of-God section must prove that ‘it is that thing that gives us the value’ refers to a real divine status rather than a humane theological label attached to human social protections.
  • The kenosis section must explain how full divinity and real non-omniscience coexist without merely moving the contradiction into the word mystery.
  • The discipleship and calling sections must distinguish truth-tracking formation from identity transmission: ‘kids mimic their parents’ and ‘He distributes ministry through gifting’ are not evidence that Christianity is true or that gifts are supernatural.

Calibration Tests

Evidence that would change the assessment

The episode gives mostly verbal reasoning from Scripture, theological tradition, anecdote, and remembered studies. That evidence can support modest internal conclusions for Christians, but it cannot by itself establish metaphysical facts about God, objective human value, supernatural gifts, or the truth of Christian doctrine.
