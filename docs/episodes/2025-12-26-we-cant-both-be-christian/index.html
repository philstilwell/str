We Can’t Both Be Christian

Episode source
Stand to Reason Weekly Podcast / Podbean
Speaker
Greg Koukl
Transcript source for this critique
Private ASR transcript generated with gpt-4o-mini-transcribe from the official episode audio; timestamps inherit the stored chunk boundaries.

This episode is strongest when it asks for semantic honesty: LDS theology and Nicene Christianity do not mean the same thing by God, Jesus, salvation, or Christian identity. That categorical point largely survives. What does not survive is the episode’s slide from definitional boundary-setting into high-confidence theological closure, selective treatment of rival revelation, scriptural proof-texting as public warrant, and psychological diagnosis of unbelief as rebellion.

1. Jesus-label insufficiency

Believing in “Jesus” does not by itself settle Christian identity

Steelmanned, Koukl’s point is a fair semantic warning: religious labels can become misleading when radically different doctrines use the same central name. A Muslim, an LDS believer, a Jehovah’s Witness, a liberal Protestant, and a Nicene evangelical may all speak positively about Jesus while assigning incompatible statuses, histories, powers, and salvific roles to him. On that narrow point, the episode is right to resist the thin formula that any favorable reference to Jesus automatically counts as Christianity.

The critique is that the episode treats a useful classification dispute as if it can carry more epistemic weight than it can. Showing that LDS Christology differs from Nicene Christology does not show that Nicene Christology is true, that LDS restorationism is false, or that either has adequate public warrant. A charitable conclusion is: “LDS theology is not a denomination of classical Nicene Christianity.” A less justified conclusion is: “therefore our Jesus is the real Jesus in the relevant metaphysical sense.” The latter needs evidence beyond label management.

Formalization

The episode’s semantic argument can be reconstructed as a conditional about category membership rather than a proof of Christianity’s truth.

\[ \[ \begin{aligned} &\forall x\,[(C(x) \rightarrow E(x))] \\ &\neg E(\mathrm{LDS}) \\ &\therefore \neg C(\mathrm{LDS}) \end{aligned} \] where \(C(x)\) means x is a denomination of classical Christianity and \(E(x)\) means x affirms the essential doctrines of classical Christianity. \]

Assessment

The form is valid if the definition of classical Christianity is granted and if LDS theology really denies one or more essentials. But the conclusion is taxonomic: LDS is not classical Christianity. It does not establish that classical Christianity is metaphysically true, publicly warranted, or more probable than LDS restorationism.

Equivocation Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The episode is largely trying to prevent equivocation on “Jesus” and “Christian,” which is legitimate. The risk is that it may replace one ambiguity with another by shifting between “Christian” as a historical-doctrinal category and “Christian” as the true saving religion.

Ingroup bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The analysis privileges the in-group’s inherited definition of Christian identity. That may be historically defensible, but the same deference would allow LDS believers to define restored Christianity around their own founding narrative, producing rival boundary claims rather than neutral adjudication.

2. Nicene boundary and Godhead conflict

The Trinity argument clarifies difference but does not verify Nicene metaphysics

Steelmanned, Koukl’s strongest evidence is textual and institutional: he quotes an LDS reference work distinguishing the Godhead as separate personages and explicitly contrasting that view with traditional Christian ontological oneness. If classical Christianity is defined by Nicene monotheism and tri-personal divine unity, then an LDS model of three divine beings is not a minor denominational variation. On the episode’s own limited question—denomination or different religion—this is the most substantial section.

The deeper problem is that Christian history itself displays interpretive struggle over exactly the doctrines Koukl treats as clean sine qua nons. That does not make every interpretation equally plausible, and it does not rescue LDS theology from contradiction. It does mean the appeal to “classical Christianity” should be kept in its lane. It can say, “this is not what the Nicene tradition means.” It cannot, without further evidence, say, “therefore the Nicene tradition has successfully described reality.”

Formalization

The core argument is a membership test tied to a doctrinal essential.

\[ \[ \begin{aligned} &\forall x\,[(D(x) \land \neg T(x)) \rightarrow \neg N(x)] \\ &D(\mathrm{LDS}) \\ &\neg T(\mathrm{LDS}) \\ &\therefore \neg N(\mathrm{LDS}) \end{aligned} \] where \(D(x)\) means x claims Christian-denominational status, \(T(x)\) means x affirms Nicene Trinitarianism, and \(N(x)\) means x is a denomination of Nicene Christianity. \]

Assessment

As a definition-governed argument, this is strong. As an argument for the existence of the triune God, it is weak because the decisive premise is classificatory. A tradition can police its boundaries without thereby showing that its boundary-making doctrines are true.

Appeal to authority Diagnostic fit: Moderate

Nicaea is relevant historical authority for defining Nicene Christianity, but it is not by itself evidence that the Trinity exists. The fallacy risk appears if conciliar status is treated as truth-making rather than tradition-defining.

Authority bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The episode leans heavily on inherited ecclesial authority to settle what counts as Christian. That may be appropriate for internal classification, but it can overawe listeners into confusing institutional pedigree with public epistemic warrant.

3. Subjective confirmation asymmetry

Koukl correctly criticizes LDS feeling-based confirmation but under-audits Christian analogues

Steelmanned, the episode is right to be suspicious of a conversion method that asks people to pray for an internal sensation and then treats that sensation as confirmation of a religious text. A “burning in the bosom” is not a public test of truth. It is psychologically vulnerable to expectation, community pressure, confirmation bias, emotional priming, and retrospective interpretation. Koukl’s criticism lands against any system that converts felt assurance into high-confidence belief.

The episode’s vulnerability is that it isolates LDS subjectivity while leaving similar Christian subjectivity mostly unexamined. Koukl calls the LDS approach “wrong,” but he later relies on biblical passages and theological interpretations whose public warrant is not independently established in the episode. The proper critique is broader: neither LDS burning, nor evangelical inner witness, nor any rival religious feeling should be allowed to bypass external calibration. Sincerity and pastoral usefulness do not turn private experience into reliable evidence.

Formalization

The criticism can be formalized as a reliability problem for subjective confirmation.

\[ \[ \begin{aligned} &\forall r\,[(S(r) \land M(r)) \rightarrow \neg R(r)] \\ &S(\mathrm{LDS}) \land M(\mathrm{LDS}) \\ &\therefore \neg R(\mathrm{LDS}) \end{aligned} \] where \(S(r)\) means religion r uses subjective confirmation as a truth test, \(M(r)\) means mutually incompatible religions can generate similar confirmations, and \(R(r)\) means the method is reliable for public truth-tracking. \]

Assessment

The structure is sound if the premises are applied consistently. The argument becomes special pleading if subjective confirmation is rejected for LDS claims but preserved for evangelical claims under different labels such as conviction, peace, witness, or divine prompting.

Hasty generalization Diagnostic fit: Moderate

Koukl suggests missionaries may not understand the doctrinal differences and that the appeal is principally subjective. That may often be true, but the transcript does not provide systematic evidence about missionary training, individual knowledge, or all LDS apologetic methods.

Confirmation bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The critique selects the weakest LDS epistemic practice while not equally foregrounding structurally similar Christian practices. That selection confirms the in-group’s rationality and the out-group’s irrationality unless the same test is applied both ways.

4. Ordained means and unfalsifiable providence

“God ordains the means” preserves evangelism but shields sovereignty from testing

Steelmanned, Koukl gives a coherent internal answer to the caller’s concern. If God ordains not only final outcomes but also the human actions that bring those outcomes about, then evangelism, gentleness, and persuasion are not irrelevant. They are the ordained instruments through which the ordained end occurs. This is a standard compatibilist move, and it preserves pastoral motivation: be a winsome ambassador because your conduct may be part of the means.

Koukl’s answer therefore works as theological counseling for Christians already inside his framework. It does not function as public evidence that divine sovereignty exists, that God draws particular people, or that Romans 8 and John 6 describe real causal mechanics. The episode should have separated “this doctrine lets ambassadors see their work as meaningful” from “this doctrine accurately explains conversion.” The first is pastoral utility; the second requires observable differentiators between divine drawing and ordinary persuasion, identity formation, social pressure, and cognitive change.

Formalization

Koukl’s reasoning is a compatibilist inference from divine ordination to the continued relevance of human action.

\[ \[ \begin{aligned} &O(E) \\ &\forall e\,[O(e) \rightarrow \exists m\,O(m \rightarrow e)] \\ &O(A \rightarrow E) \\ &\therefore \neg I(A) \end{aligned} \] where \(O\) means God ordains, \(E\) means a person’s salvation, \(A\) means winsome ambassadorship, and \(I(A)\) means A is irrelevant. \]

Assessment

The argument is valid inside the theological system if one grants divine ordination of both ends and means. Its public weakness is that the premises are asserted from scripture and doctrine, while the model lacks clear empirical conditions under which it would be judged false.

Begging the question Diagnostic fit: High

The answer assumes the very providential structure at issue: that God ordains ends and means. That may be biblical theology for insiders, but it cannot answer the public explanatory question unless God’s ordaining activity is independently supported.

Motivated reasoning Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The explanation protects two desired commitments at once: God decisively saves, and evangelistic effort still matters. The reconciliation may be coherent, but it is also shaped to preserve both doctrines regardless of observable conversion outcomes.

5. Miracle evidence and rebellion diagnosis

The Lazarus example turns evidential failure into a heart accusation

Steelmanned, Koukl is making a psychologically plausible point: evidence alone does not mechanically compel belief. Human beings can resist uncomfortable conclusions, reinterpret threatening data, protect status, and act against what appears rational. In that modest sense, the Lazarus and Paul examples illustrate a real feature of cognition: even dramatic events would be filtered through prior commitments, fears, incentives, and social identity.

The most damaging move is “rank rebellion.” Once unbelief is interpreted as rebellion even in the face of miracle claims, the apologetic becomes insulated: if evidence persuades, Christianity is confirmed; if evidence does not persuade, the skeptic’s heart is blamed. That move may reinforce believers pastorally, but it does not improve public warrant. A responsible version would say only that some people may resist evidence for non-evidential reasons. It should not use biblical miracle stories to diagnose contemporary nonbelief without independent access to the facts and motives involved.

Formalization

The episode’s inference moves from narrative miracle rejection to a broader claim about unbelief and evidence.

\[ \[ \begin{aligned} &R(L) \\ &K(P,L) \\ &\neg B(P) \\ &\therefore Reb(P) \end{aligned} \] where \(R(L)\) means Lazarus was raised, \(K(P,L)\) means the Pharisees knew this, \(B(P)\) means the Pharisees believed rightly, and \(Reb(P)\) means their unbelief was rebellion. \]

Assessment

The inference depends on several heavy premises not established in the episode: that the Lazarus event occurred, that the leaders knew it occurred, that the narrative preserves their psychology accurately, and that rebellion rather than fear, politics, theological disagreement, or literary construction best explains the story.

Hasty generalization Diagnostic fit: High

From a small set of scriptural narratives, the episode draws a broad lesson about how people respond to dramatic divine evidence. Without independent historical controls, the move risks overgeneralizing from the theology of the text to real-world unbelief.

Confirmation bias Diagnostic fit: High

The interpretation protects Christian belief from evidential challenge: acceptance of miracles confirms revelation, while rejection of miracle claims can be reframed as rebellion. That structure preferentially confirms the in-group conclusion.

Overall Assessment

Overall assessment: a strong category correction weakened by apologetic overreach

What survives charitably is the episode’s central taxonomic warning: LDS theology and classical Nicene Christianity are not merely two denominations with minor internal differences. On God, Jesus, revelation, authority, and salvation, the differences are deep enough that Koukl is justified in rejecting the simplistic slogan “we believe in Jesus too” as a sufficient denominational argument.

What must fall is the confidence that this category correction vindicates Christianity itself. The episode provides real reasons to distinguish LDS theology from Nicene Christianity, but not sufficient public evidence that Nicene metaphysics, divine drawing, biblical miracle narratives, or Jesus-only salvation claims are true. The responsible confidence level is high for “not the same doctrinal system,” moderate for “not a denomination of classical Christianity,” and low for the larger supernatural claims not independently defended here.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality

The dark epistemic reality is that this episode repeatedly wins easier fights than the one it needs to win. It can show that LDS theology is not Nicene. It can show that private burning sensations are unreliable. It can show that labels need boundaries. But none of that licenses high-confidence belief in a triune deity, divine election, historical resurrections, or the claim that rejection of Jesus reveals rejection of God.

The harshest asymmetry is the treatment of faith and subjectivity. LDS inner confirmation is rightly downgraded, but Christian confidence is allowed to lean on scripture, theological interpretation, and inherited authority without undergoing the same public calibration. If faith means relational trust after adequate evidence, it may describe commitment. If faith means confidence beyond evidence, it is not an epistemic virtue. It is the defect the episode notices in Mormonism and insufficiently notices in itself.

  • The LDS-vs-Nicene boundary survives; the leap from boundary to truth does not.
  • Subjective spiritual confirmation should be rejected across the board, not only when it belongs to the out-group.
  • Providence, divine drawing, and rebellion diagnoses remain internally useful but publicly weak unless they generate testable differentiators.

The challenge

The challenge: prove the Christian claims, or stop hiding behind LDS failures

The dark red challenge is this: stop treating Mormonism’s problems as if they pay Christianity’s evidential bills. When the episode says “we believe in Jesus, you believe in Jesus” is too thin, it is right. But when it pivots from that to inherited Nicene confidence, the bill comes due. Show that the triune God exists. Show that the New Testament reliably reveals him. Show that the Lazarus and resurrection narratives are more than sectarian miracle literature. If those cannot be shown, then the episode has not defended Christianity; it has only defended a trademark boundary.

The weakest move is the rebellion shield. “It was just rank rebellion against what had been revealed” is not an argument; it is an accusation resting on the very miracle story under dispute. Likewise, “God ordains the means as well as the end” explains every outcome after the fact and risks explaining nothing before the fact. The episode must prove that these are real causal descriptions of the world, not devotional captions placed over ordinary persuasion, group identity, and ancient narrative. If it cannot, it should retract the confidence and speak more modestly.

  • On the LDS critique: after “because their beliefs about Jesus are so different,” prove that your Jesus is historically and metaphysically real rather than merely definitionally orthodox.
  • On subjective confirmation: after mocking “this burning in the bosom,” apply the same standard to evangelical inner witness, conviction, providence, and faith-based assurance, or admit the special pleading.
  • On miracle resistance: after “Jesus just raised somebody from the dead,” provide independent historical evidence for the event and the opponents’ knowledge state, or stop using the story to indict unbelievers as rebels.
