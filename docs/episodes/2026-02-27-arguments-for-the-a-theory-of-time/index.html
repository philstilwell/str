1. A-theory from lived passage

Experience of passage is evidence, not a verdict

Steelman: Kokel’s best point is that A-theory captures the ordinary phenomenology of life. We experience anticipation, present attention, memory, decision, repentance, and hope as genuinely tensed. If a theory of time makes that entire structure illusory or derivative, then it carries an explanatory cost that should be counted. His audience-facing illustration of sequential events also gives non-specialists a real handle on why the issue matters.

The critique is not that A-theory is irrational. The critique is that the episode treats intuitive fit as if it substantially defeats B-theory. A B-theorist need not say there are no events; they may say events are ordered by earlier-than and later-than relations without an objectively moving present. Kokel’s “nothing happens” framing risks attacking a caricature: it replaces the opponent’s denial of objective becoming with the stronger and less plausible claim that no event-relations exist at all.

Research anchors Binary Thinking Epistemological Basics The two selected epistemic analyses are relevant because the episode repeatedly shifts between binary dismissal and informal abduction. The first cautions against treating a complex probabilistic dispute as a simple A-or-B verdict. The second clarifies deductive, inductive, and abductive moves, which matters because Kokel’s appeal to experience is abductive at best, not a deductive refutation of B-theory.

Formalization

A charitable reconstruction treats experience of passage as evidence for A-theory, while marking the overreach from evidence to dismissal.

\[ \text{If }E_{passage}\text{ is our lived experience of temporal flow, then }P(A\mid E_{passage})>P(A).\ \text{But }P(A\mid E_{passage})>P(B\mid E_{passage})\not\Rightarrow P(A\mid E_{all})\approx 1. \]

Assessment

The first step is reasonable: experience can raise A-theory’s plausibility. The invalid step is treating that localized support as a broad defeat of B-theory without comparing all relevant evidence and the strongest B-theoretic explanations.

Hasty generalization Diagnostic fit: High

The episode generalizes from the felt passage of time and a simplified book analogy to the conclusion that B-theory is mistaken and amounts to nothing happening. That is too quick for a contested metaphysical dispute.

Selection bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

The argument selects the data most favorable to A-theory, especially ordinary experience and biblical-sounding sequence, while setting aside the strongest reasons philosophers or physicists might consider tenseless models.

2. Divine action as temporal proof

Biblical sequence does not automatically settle God’s ontology

Steelman: Kokel is pressing a real pressure point for classical atemporality. If God creates, speaks, enters history in Jesus, responds, promises, and later brings about a second coming, then God appears to be involved in ordered events. A view of God that cannot make sense of divine agency, responsiveness, or covenantal interaction seems pastorally and conceptually thin.

The transcript also relies on scripture as if narrative sequence directly maps onto divine metaphysics. Biblical faith-language and action-language often functions relationally: trust, reliance, loyalty, warning, and promise. That language can matter deeply without serving as technical evidence for A-theory divine temporality. Treating narrative depictions as metaphysical instruction risks over-reading genre and importing theological confidence where only interpretive plausibility has been supplied.

A theological system may organize biblical action-language elegantly, but that does not itself prove the metaphysical structure it assigns to God. The overreach concern is strongest where the transcript moves from coherence within Christian narration to public confidence about divine temporality.

The central inference can be formalized as a conditional about divine action and temporality; the contested premise is where the weight rests.

\[ \forall x\big((A_T(x)\lor E_T(x))\rightarrow T(x)\big),\ A_T(G)\therefore T(G).\ \text{The dispute is whether temporal actions or effects require }T(G). \]

Assessment

The argument is valid if the major premise is granted, but the major premise is the very issue under debate. Without defending it against timeless-action models, the conclusion remains a theological preference rather than a demonstrated result.

Equivocation Diagnostic fit: High

The argument slides between God producing temporal effects, God being described in temporal narratives, and God possessing temporal succession in his own life. Those are related but not equivalent claims.

Confirmation bias Diagnostic fit: High

The transcript highlights biblical passages and concepts that support a temporal reading while giving little serious space to alternative theological interpretations that would preserve divine action without divine succession.

3. Creation as first temporal act

The first-act solution names the transition problem; it does not dissolve it

Steelman: Kokel gives a clean answer to Tom’s objection. If time begins with creation, then God does not first act inside an already existing timeline. Creation is the first temporal event, and asking what happened before time may be a malformed question. This is a respectable way to avoid imagining a temporal waiting room prior to the universe.

The episode’s phrase “the minute he acted” is rhetorically clear but philosophically dangerous. Minutes belong to time. If it is only a figure of speech, fine; then the model needs more precision. If it is doing explanatory work, it presupposes what it is trying to explain. The better posture is modesty: this proposal may be one coherent theological option, but the transcript has not shown that it is less mysterious than timeless creation or B-theoretic eternalism.

The biconditional captures the proposal, but the final condition exposes the pressure point. If acts require time, creation cannot be the first act in the needed sense; if acts do not require time, Kokel’s broader argument that divine action entails temporality weakens.

4. Middle knowledge and future facts

Molinist categories do not yet explain A-theory foreknowledge

Steelman: Kokel helpfully separates possible truths, actual truths, and counterfactuals of creaturely freedom. That taxonomy can prevent confusion, especially when listeners conflate omniscience, determinism, and God’s knowledge of unrealized alternatives. His point that Molinism is not automatically a B-theory rescue is also fair: middle knowledge is primarily about counterfactuals and providence, not simply about whether all times equally exist.

Kokel’s reliance on William Lane Craig also does more atmospheric work than argumentative work. Craig being an A-theorist and Molinist shows compatibility within one philosopher’s system; it does not show that the system succeeds. A public argument must do more than name categories and authorities. It must show how future-tense truths, libertarian freedom, and divine certainty can coexist without simply redescribing the mystery as middle knowledge.

The formalization shows why simply asserting divine knowledge is insufficient. If the counterfactual has a truthmaker, the model must identify it; if it lacks one, calling it knowledge becomes epistemically unstable.

Begging the question Diagnostic fit: High

Authority bias Diagnostic fit: Moderate

Craig’s role as the teacher and exemplar lends credibility to the compatibility of A-theory and Molinism, but the transcript does not independently establish that his framework answers the relevant objections.

5. Sermon borrowing and pastoral transparency

Generosity with ideas does not erase disclosure duties

Steelman: Kokel’s charitable instinct is admirable. Teaching is cumulative, pastors are overloaded, and Christian instruction has always relied on inherited language, illustrations, commentaries, mentors, and shared formulations. If a speaker explicitly grants permission for others to use his material, then reuse can multiply the good effects of the teaching. Oral communication also cannot carry scholarly footnotes for every remembered phrase.

The episode’s distinction between writing and speaking is too soft. Written plagiarism is easier to document, but oral presentation can still mislead. If a pastor says or implies that God personally laid a message on his heart while delivering a purchased or copied sermon almost verbatim, the concern is not originality fetishism. The concern is audience deception and the erosion of confidence in pastoral judgment. A simple disclosure—“this series is adapted from another church’s material”—would preserve collaboration without pretending authorship.

Overall Assessment

What survives: A-theory remains live, but the episode’s confidence must fall

Charitably, several claims survive in moderated form. A-theory really does fit ordinary temporal experience. Divine temporality is a coherent Christian option. Calling creation the first temporal act is a possible model. Middle knowledge is a useful taxonomy within some theological systems. Sermon borrowing can be legitimate when permission, adaptation, and transparency are present.

What must fall is the episode’s stronger confidence. B-theory is not shown to be “bad” or equivalent to nothing happening. Biblical sequence is not shown to settle God’s metaphysical relation to time. The first-temporal-act answer does not fully explain atemporal agency. Middle knowledge does not automatically ground future free truths. Pastoral generosity with ideas does not erase disclosure norms for near-verbatim sermons.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality: intuition dressed as certainty is still overbelief

The dark epistemic reality is that the episode repeatedly treats conceptual comfort as if it were argument-completing evidence. “This feels natural,” “Scripture sounds sequential,” “Craig holds this,” and “I see no difficulty” may orient a conversation, but they do not finish it. A listener is being invited to occupy a confident Christian metaphysical stance without being shown the full comparative cost of rival options.

Faith, where it functions as confidence beyond supplied evidence, is not an epistemic virtue here. Trusting a theological picture because it feels biblical, pastoral, or familiar is a humanly understandable posture, but it is not public warrant. Relational trust-language inside Christianity should not be inflated into evidence that the metaphysics are true.

  • A-theory should be held as a live, defensible option, not as an obvious result of ordinary experience alone.
  • Divine temporality should be presented as an interpretive-theological model, not as something forced by every divine action statement in scripture.
  • Sermon borrowing should be judged by concrete public factors—permission, attribution, representation, adaptation, and congregational expectation—not by vague appeals to ministry usefulness.

The challenge

The dark red challenge: stop calling the hard parts obvious

The weakest move in the transcript is the repeated conversion of a puzzle into a slogan. “If God is doing anything” becomes proof that “he is acting in a temporal fashion,” while “creation is his first temporal act” is treated as if it dissolves the boundary problem. But those two claims are in tension. If action entails temporality, then creation cannot easily be the first temporal act without already presupposing what it is meant to generate. The episode must either prove that action can be non-temporal at the boundary or retract the broad claim that divine action straightforwardly entails divine temporality.

The second weakest move is rhetorical downgrading of rivals and concerns: B-theory is the “bad theory,” physicalists are discomforted by passage, middle knowledge is “nothing controversial,” and near-verbatim sermon borrowing is softened with “Hey, steal my talk.” That is not careful public reasoning. It is confidence management for insiders. If the episode wants to defeat B-theory, it must answer the strongest tenseless account rather than the claim that nothing happens. If it wants to bless sermon reuse, it must prove permission and disclosure, not merely celebrate usefulness.

  • The A-theory section must answer the quote “this passage of time is discomforting to them” by either evidencing that motive claim or retracting it as psychologizing opponents.
  • The divine temporality section must reconcile “he is acting in a temporal fashion” with “creation is his first temporal act,” because together they expose the unresolved transition problem.
  • The sermon section must qualify “Hey, steal my talk” with enforceable transparency conditions, or it becomes cover for pastors presenting borrowed labor as personal spiritual discernment.

◉ Narrative Closure: Analyze whether a promised ending functions as a circular story-ending premise that predetermines the interpretation of present events.
◉ Insider Authority: Examine whether appeals to Scripture and Christian tradition establish the claims only for insiders who already grant those sources authority.
◉ Resurrection Evidence Gap: Evaluate moves from mentioning evidence to treating resurrection as a central reality when the actual evidence is not presented.
◉ Lordship Claim Expansion: Assess whether lordship claims are argued as public facts or merely proclaimed as theological commitments.
◉ Equivocation Risk: Check for shifts in the meanings of worldview, hope, truth, Lord, good, fallen, redeemed, identity, and calling.
◉ Cultural-Moment Framing: Examine whether culture-war examples create a false dilemma, strawman, or asymmetric framing.
◉ Analogy Limits: Test whether literary or pastoral analogies legitimately support public conclusions.
◉ Historical Selectivity: Evaluate uses of church-history examples for cherry-picking, survivorship bias, halo effect, or hasty generalization.
◉ Normative-Claim Threshold: Ask whether theological categories generate determinate conduct guidance without additional contested premises.
◉ Inductive Symmetry: Compare the standards used to accept Christian explanatory claims with the standards required for rival worldviews.
◉ Scope Leakage: Identify moves from this helps Christian students live with hope and purpose to therefore Christianity is true or uniquely adequate.
◉ Burden of Proof and Special Pleading: Determine whether rival views are asked to justify themselves while Christian claims are exempted from comparable scrutiny.
◉ Non Sequitur Risk: Identify conclusions that do not follow from premises, especially from prosocial motivation to metaphysical truth or from scriptural narrative to public epistemic warrant.
