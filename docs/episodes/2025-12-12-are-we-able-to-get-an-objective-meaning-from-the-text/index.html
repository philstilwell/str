Are We Able to Get an Objective Meaning from the Text?

Stand to Reason Weekly Podcast / Podbean

This episode’s strongest moments come when Greg separates textual meaning from whether the text is true, and when he tries to clarify whether a caller is affirming, denying, or suspending judgment about God. Those are useful distinctions. But the episode repeatedly turns modest distinctions into overconfident conclusions: secular provenance becomes suspicion, selected clear passages become broad biblical clarity, atheist self-description becomes evasion, and moral non-realism becomes practical paralysis. The charitable remainder is narrower than the rhetoric suggests.

1. Secular-origin suspicion toward hermeneutics

A red flag is not an argument against the spiral

Steelmanned, Greg’s concern is intelligible: if a hermeneutical model is shaped by secular academy trends that often relativize meaning, pastors should ask whether the model will weaken confidence in authorial intent. Carter describes an interpretive loop in which presuppositions affect reading, which then revises presuppositions, and Greg worries that this could become a theological import from institutions not committed to Scripture’s authority. That is a pastoral caution, not nothing. A Christian teacher may reasonably ask whether a method is helping readers correct bias or merely licensing skepticism.

The critique is that this inference remains underpowered. A method can be taught in secular universities and still help readers notice bias, compare interpretations, and converge on authorial meaning. Conversely, a method taught in conservative seminaries can still protect in-group assumptions from scrutiny. The decisive question is not whether outsiders like the method, but whether the method improves interpretive accuracy under adversarial testing. Greg’s red-flag heuristic may be pastorally understandable, but it cannot carry the confidence he assigns to calling the trend probably unhealthy.

2. Biblical clarity and authorial meaning

Clear examples cannot carry a doctrine of plain meaning

Steelmanned, Greg makes an important distinction: interpreting what a text claims is not the same as believing the claim is true. His example of a secular interviewer agreeing that certain passages teach what he says they teach, while rejecting the teaching, is a legitimate hermeneutical distinction. A reader can say, “This is what the author seems to assert,” and then separately ask whether the assertion is historically, ethically, or metaphysically credible. That distinction protects discussion from collapsing into mere preference.

The critique is that the episode uses familiar clear passages to carry a much heavier conclusion about access to biblical meaning. If a divine or uniquely authoritative text is claimed to guide salvation, ethics, and communal life, then widespread, durable disagreement among sincere and competent readers is not a minor inconvenience; it is evidence that clarity is uneven. Biblical faith-language can express trust, reliance, or loyalty after one has accepted a tradition, but it cannot repair public warrant when interpretive ambiguity remains. Confidence in the Bible’s “plain things” must be calibrated by what readers actually converge on, not by what one tradition experiences as obvious.

3. Atheism as denial versus non-affirmation

The law of excluded middle does not settle label use

Steelmanned, Greg is trying to prevent equivocation. If “atheist” means one thing in a philosophical article and another thing in a caller’s self-description, both parties can talk past each other. His three-option structure—agree, disagree, or withhold—also usefully distinguishes belief that God exists, belief that God does not exist, and suspension of judgment. That is a real clarification, especially when people use labels as social identities rather than precise epistemic reports.

The critique is that lack of belief is not logically equivalent to belief in the negation. Greg repeatedly presses David toward the classical definition, but David’s “withhold” answer is coherent if understood as an attitude report rather than a metaphysical thesis. One can lack belief in God because the evidence has not crossed an assent threshold without affirming that no gods exist. In public argument, the useful question is not whether the label atheist is classical enough; it is what credence the person assigns to God’s existence, what evidence would move it, and what positive claims they are actually making.

The law of excluded middle applies to G, not to the claim that every non-theist must believe not-G. David’s suspension is logically coherent even if Greg prefers a different label for it.

Illusion of explanatory depth Diagnostic fit: Moderate

4. Burden of proof and lack-belief atheism

Writing against theism does not automatically assert no gods exist

Steelmanned, Greg has a legitimate complaint against a real rhetorical tactic: some public atheists argue vigorously against theism while describing themselves as merely lacking belief when asked for their own burden of proof. If a writer asserts that God does not exist, or that naturalism is probably true, then that writer has evidential responsibilities. Criticism does not become rationally cost-free just because it is framed negatively. Greg is right that public argument should not hide positive claims behind minimalist definitions.

The critique is that “Nobody writes books about their non-beliefs” is rhetorically punchy but epistemically sloppy. People do write about nonbeliefs when those beliefs dominate institutions, shape laws, affect families, or impose costs. A secular critic of Christianity may be arguing that Christian arguments fail, that religious institutions do harm, or that believers overstate confidence. Those are not identical to proving there is no God. Greg’s burden concern should be applied claim-by-claim, not label-by-label: each speaker must support the positive content they actually assert.

5. Ethical Anti-Realism and LGBTQ objection

Non-allegedly objective conduct norms is not no public objection

Steelmanned, Greg identifies a real implication of Ethical Anti-Realism: if David denies stance-independent stance-independent norm claims, then he cannot consistently say anti-LGBTQ mistreatment violates an objective divine command norm built into reality. From Greg’s perspective, David’s objection becomes an expression of evolved and culturally shaped concern rather than a discovery of an objective ethical property. That is a legitimate metaethical pressure point, and David partly concedes it by saying his ethical discourse is subjective rather than imposed on him by the world.

The critique is that Greg frames David as stuck saying mistreatment is merely wrong-for-me and right-for-you, as if all non-objective normativity collapses into private taste. But societies regularly adjudicate conflicts through intersubjective standards: harm evidence, psychological outcomes, equal protection, institutional trust, and the practical costs of exclusion. A Christian ethical realist still has to explain why divine-command-like authority yields a coherent public system, especially when Christians themselves disagree and when biblical texts require interpretation. Moral non-realism does not give David cosmic furniture; it gives him public reasons. That is enough for objection, policy, and condemnation within human normative life.

Overall Assessment

What survives: clarity distinctions, not apologetic overconfidence

The charitable residue is real. Greg is right that interpretation and belief are distinct: a reader can understand what a passage claims while denying that the claim is true. He is also right that positive claims bear burdens, whether made by Christians, atheists, or ethical critics. And he is right that moral anti-realists should not smuggle in objective ethical realism while denying it. Those distinctions are useful and worth preserving.

But the confidence levels must fall in four places. The secular-origin suspicion of the hermeneutical spiral is too thin to justify calling it probably unhealthy. The appeal to biblical perspicuity is too broad for the evidence supplied. The attempt to make atheism classically denial-only does not adequately handle credence, suspension, and contemporary label use. And the treatment of moral non-realism wrongly implies that without objective stance-independent norm claims there are no real human grounds for objection at all. Each point has a defensible core, but each core is wrapped in overstatement.

The epistemic reality

The epistemic reality: the episode mistakes control of terms for control of truth

The dark epistemic reality is that this episode often wins by narrowing the vocabulary before doing the evidential work. Call the hermeneutical spiral secular, call biblical clarity plain, call lack-belief atheism evasive, call non-allegedly objective conduct norms groundless, and the Christian position looks cleaner than it has earned. But naming the frame is not the same as proving the frame. A rational listener should not let a red flag substitute for data or a classical definition substitute for credence mapping.

Faith, where it functions as confidence beyond the evidence, does not repair any of these gaps. Biblical faith-language may express trust, reliance, or loyalty within a relationship or tradition, but it cannot publicly warrant a contested interpretation, a metaphysical claim, or a normative framework. The episode’s pastoral confidence may be sincere and useful for insiders, but sincerity and usefulness are not evidence that the arguments have crossed the public warrant threshold.

  • A method’s origin is not evidence of its unreliability unless a mechanism and outcome data are supplied.
  • Some clear biblical passages do not demonstrate broad doctrinal clarity across competent, sincere interpreters.
  • Non-allegedly objective conduct norms removes stance-independent stance-independent norm claims, not human reasons grounded in harm, consent, cooperation, and public justification.

The challenge

The challenge: prove the overstatements or retract the pressure tactics

The weakest points in the transcript are not minor slips; they are load-bearing shortcuts. When Greg says the hermeneutical spiral is a red flag because of secular academic influence, he must prove that the method corrupts interpretation, not merely that outsiders use it. When he says “the main things are the plain things,” he must show durable convergence on those main things, not just cite passages his own tradition already treats as obvious. If he cannot provide that evidence, the charge that the spiral breeds distrust should be retracted or reduced to a personal caution.

The ethical exchange is the sharpest failure. Greg presses David toward the claim that, without allegedly objective conduct norms, “there is no grounds for real objection between you.” That is not a refutation of moral non-realism; it is a false squeeze. David does not need cosmic ethical furniture to object to anti-LGBTQ mistreatment. He needs public reasons: harm, exclusion, hypocrisy relative to professed compassion, and social standards that protect vulnerable people. If Greg wants to say those are not “real” objections, he must prove that only stance-independent stance-independent norm claims count as real reasons. Otherwise the episode should retract the insinuation that secular ethical criticism is just private taste wearing borrowed authority.

  • On the hermeneutical spiral, prove that the method itself produces worse authorial-intent judgments than traditional approaches, or stop treating secular uptake as evidence of theological contamination.
  • On atheism, prove that David’s suspended credence logically entails belief that God does not exist, or concede that label disputes are secondary to mapping actual claims and burdens.
  • On ethical discourse, prove that harm-based public reasons are not real reasons unless objective stance-independent norm claims exist, or retract the claim that David has no real grounds to object to anti-LGBTQ mistreatment.
